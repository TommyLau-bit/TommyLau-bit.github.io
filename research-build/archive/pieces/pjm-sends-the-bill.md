In the summer of 2024, the price in PJM's capacity auction rose almost tenfold in a single year. Every auction since has cleared at the highest price the rules allow.

PJM runs the high-voltage grid for 67 million people across 13 states and Washington DC, including the data centre cluster in northern Virginia.

Its auction does not buy electricity. It buys a promise that power stations will be ready on the hardest days, years ahead.

Data centres are raising how much promise PJM must buy faster than anyone can build the plants. Households pay the difference.

PJM's auction cannot summon new power stations in time. It can only send the bill.

## How the auction sets the bill

Each year PJM forecasts its peak demand a few years out, adds a safety margin and auctions for that much capacity. Capacity is a promise, paid by the day, that a power station will run when the grid is under most strain.

The last megawatt needed sets the price for every megawatt. Each utility pays its share and passes it into household bills.

Data centres enter the forecast before they are built. PJM's market monitor counts more than 21 gigawatts of data centre load in the latest one. As the forecast rises, so does what PJM must buy.

Supply cannot simply catch up. A large power station needs a turbine, a grid connection and years of studies. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory finds the median large project takes nearly five years from asking to connect to switching on. PJM's auctions now run less than three years ahead, so a high price arrives too late to summon a plant.

After Pennsylvania's governor complained to federal regulators, PJM put a temporary cap and floor on the price. A cap holds the price down. It does not add a megawatt.

## The auction that came up short

The July 2026 auction cleared at the cap for the third time running. Its total value was $16.4 billion.

It also fell almost 7 gigawatts short of PJM's own reliability requirement. New power stations and upgrades added only 525 megawatts. PJM's independent market monitor attributes nearly two fifths of the auction's value to data centre load.

Households feel it first. After an earlier auction, New Jersey's regulator expected household bills to rise by 17 to 20 per cent from June 2025, citing data centre demand and slow connections for new plants.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if a PJM auction clears below $325 per megawatt-day, today's cap, and meets its reliability requirement while the data centre forecast is still rising. That would mean price alone had summoned enough supply.

I am also wrong if new power stations clearing an auction start to outrun the forecast's added data centre load.
