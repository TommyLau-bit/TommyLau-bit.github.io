Ask most people what a grid battery is for and you get the same answer. Store electricity when it is cheap, release it when it is scarce.

That is a real job and batteries still do it.

It is no longer the job that explains who is buying them.

The battery has been quietly reassigned. It is becoming a piece of connection equipment.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Think about moving into a flat where the gas has not been connected yet. You want to cook tonight, not in four months when the engineer finally comes.</p>
<p>So you buy a camping stove. You did not want a camping stove. You bought it because it lets you start living in the flat before the utility is ready for you.</p>
</details>

## Three jobs, and only one is the famous one

**Getting open early.** A site with a partial grid connection can draw only a limited amount of power continuously. A battery lets it run harder for part of the day by discharging, then refill slowly when demand is low. The building opens and starts earning while the wires are still being finished.

**Absorbing the violence.** AI workloads swing hard and fast. A battery between the grid and the computers smooths some of that, which matters now that grid operators treat sudden load changes as a reliability problem.

**Buying low and selling high.** The original job, and increasingly the least interesting reason anyone writes the cheque.

A battery is a bridge, not a connection. It changes when a site can start operating, which is valuable, but it does not make the substation or transformer arrive sooner. You do not buy a bridge unless the gap is real.

## The storage is following the load

The United States gives the clearest picture because the data is published.

Developers planned to add 24 gigawatts of utility-scale battery storage in 2026. **12.9 gigawatts of that, 53 per cent of the national total, is in Texas.**

Texas is not half the American economy. It is where an enormous amount of new computing load is arriving, on a grid separate from the rest of the country. The storage is following the load, not the electricity price.

A developer buying a battery to open eight months early is not comparing it to the cost of stored electricity. They are comparing it to eight months of earning nothing.

## What would prove me wrong

The clean falsifier is location. If new storage stops clustering where the computing load is, and spreads out with renewable generation instead, the buyer I describe is smaller than I think.

I would also be wrong if connection waits shorten enough that opening early stops being worth paying for.
