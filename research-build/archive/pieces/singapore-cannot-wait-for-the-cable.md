In August, Singapore handed four operators 200 megawatts of new data centre capacity. The rules asked for at least half their power to be green, and all four promised more.

The routes the rules name are clean fuels, used on Jurong Island, an industrial island off the coast, and solar panels fixed to walls. A cable is not among them.

That is odd, because Singapore's own plan for clean electricity rests on cables. Its regulator wants about 6 gigawatts of imports by 2035.

My reading: Singapore's data centres cannot wait for the cable, so they make green power at home.

<p>You want a new chest freezer, and you want it to run on clean power. Your flat has no roof to speak of. A friend across the road has solar panels to spare, but a new cable must be made to order, laid under the road and approved by two councils.</p>
<p>So the landlord says you may plug in the freezer if you bring half its power in bottles of clean gas. The cable is still the real plan. It will just not arrive in time for the freezer.</p>

## Why the cable runs on a slow clock

Singapore has almost no spare land, so it cannot cover fields in solar panels or wind turbines. About 95 per cent of its electricity comes from burning natural gas.

Its answer is to import clean power. A little already arrives over the existing link to Malaysia, which carries up to 1 gigawatt. The Energy Market Authority, the power regulator, has also approved 13 new import projects from Indonesia, Malaysia, Vietnam, Cambodia and Australia.

All 13 are conditional. A project gets a full importer licence, the permission to build, only once it has approvals in each country, buyers and money.

Then the physical work starts. Each project needs a subsea cable, an armoured power line laid along the seabed, and a converter station at each end. The converter turns the direct current that travels well under water into the alternating current a city grid uses.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Cable and converters are built to order for each route, and [their makers already plan queues years long](/journal/hitachi-energy-plans-the-queue/).

## What Singapore's own plans show

The data centre call is the first test. It asked for at least 50 per cent of each new site's power from pathways such as biomethane, low-carbon ammonia and low-carbon hydrogen. Imported electricity is not mentioned.

The regulator's new five-year plan is the second. Five new gas power stations will add about a quarter to the island's generating capacity by 2031. For imports, the plan offers landing sites where cables come ashore, and studies for a second link to Malaysia.

The developers' own dates run from 2028 at the earliest to 2035 for the newest approval. Neither agency says why imports were left out of the call. I read it as a government that [already rations data centre power](/journal/two-governments-one-confession/) declining to lean on cables not yet built.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if any of the conditionally approved import projects delivers power into Singapore before 1 January 2029, over a new cable or a second Malaysia link. Power over today's link from older schemes does not count.

I am also wrong if the next data centre call lets newly imported electricity count towards its green share. Then the data centres were waiting on the cable after all.
