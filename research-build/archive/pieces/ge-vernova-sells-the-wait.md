In the first half of this year, GE Vernova signed contracts for 41 gigawatts of gas turbines. It shipped seven.

Most of what it signed was not an order for a machine. It was a paid place in the line, much of it for the end of the decade.

That money is stretching the factories GE Vernova already has. It is not building a new one.

GE Vernova is not failing to clear its queue. The queue is the product, and the customers are paying for it.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a workshop that makes the best sofas in town, with a waiting list well into next year. It starts taking deposits to hold a delivery week, and spends them on a second sewing machine and longer shifts, not a second workshop.</p>
<p>Your deposit bought you a place in the line. It did not make the line shorter.</p>
</details>

## How the queue works

GE Vernova makes the large gas turbines at the centre of power stations, each producing hundreds of megawatts. It also makes smaller ones adapted from jet engines, which are quicker to ship.

Buyers can sign a slot reservation, a paid hold on a future production slot, before they are ready to order a machine. GE Vernova counts orders and reservations together, and at the end of June the total stood at 116 gigawatts.

From the second half of this year it ships about 20 gigawatts a year. So the queue is almost six years of output, and the company says it is mostly sold out to the end of the decade. A turbine shipped in 2030 can then need another year and a half on site before it makes power.

It will not simply build another factory. Its plan raises output by half from the floors it has, and the pace is set by castings and forgings, the large shaped metal parts at a turbine's hot heart. A factory runs for decades, and a maker that remembers past slumps prefers a long queue to an empty building. I read that as deliberate, and rational.

## Where the deposits show up

If customers are paying to hold a place, the cash should show before the revenue does. It does.

The money GE Vernova holds for gas turbines not yet delivered rose by about two thirds in six months, to $27.7 billion at the end of June. Its own filing gives the reason: higher down payments on orders and reservations. Its chief executive calls the expansion "all funded by customer down payments".

A buyer who pays years early is not buying a turbine. It is buying a date.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if, before 2030, the queue falls below four years of GE Vernova's planned output. Today it is almost six.

I am also wrong if GE Vernova announces a new heavy-duty turbine factory, or if its deposits and prices fall while orders stay strong. Either would mean buyers no longer need to pay to queue.
