On 6 October, Constellation agreed to sell Google the output of 890 megawatts of new nuclear capacity, roughly one large reactor's worth.

No reactor is being built. The power will come from upgrading eleven reactors Constellation already runs, in Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey.

The site, the licence and the place on the grid are already there. The new equipment, the regulator's approval and a free slot to fit it are not.

Constellation's new megawatts will not arrive as one reactor. They arrive one refuelling outage at a time.

<p>Picture fitting a bigger engine to a delivery van that is on the road every day. The van, the driver and the parking space are already yours, but the engine must be ordered, the van must pass a fresh inspection, and the swap waits for its booked day at the garage.</p>
<p>Do that across a whole fleet and the extra power trickles in van by van, never all at once.</p>

## How an uprate works

An uprate raises the output of a reactor that is already running. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the federal safety regulator, sorts them by size.

The smallest kind adds under 2 per cent, by measuring the water fed to the reactor more precisely so it can run closer to its limit. The largest, extended uprates, have been approved for up to 20 per cent. They need new high-pressure turbines, pumps, generators or transformers.

Constellation is also fitting better turbines, which turn more of each reactor's heat into electricity. Either way, the work shares one gate. Heavy equipment can only be swapped while the reactor is shut down to refuel, and Constellation's units refuel once every 18 to 24 months. Any uprate that raises the reactor's heat also needs the regulator to amend its licence first.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Constellation cannot add shutdowns without losing the power it already sells, so each unit offers one window every year and a half to two years. Miss it, and those megawatts wait for the next.

## Where the outages show up

The regulator keeps a list of the uprate applications that operators expect to file. Its September edition shows a dozen from Constellation, the first due in late 2026.

Seven of the twelve are the smallest kind. Most of the extended uprates, which carry the bulk of the megawatts, will not even be filed until late 2027 or after, and the last not until late 2030.

That calendar matches what the two companies said. Constellation expects the first uprate by 2028, and Google says all 890 megawatts reach the grid before the end of 2032.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Constellation reports more than half of the 890 megawatts in service by the end of 2029. That would mean its steps came faster than the outage calendar and the regulator's list allow.

I am also wrong if Constellation files every extended uprate application by the end of 2027, pulling its last steps years forward.
