Since July 2025, Malaysia's data centres have been billed in a tariff class of their own. Each unit of electricity costs them about a quarter more than it costs an ordinary large factory.

That sounds like data centres paying for the grid built for them. Look at the bill line by line and they are not.

The premium sits on the energy. The charges for the wires are the same as any other large user's.

Malaysia charges data centres more for power. The wires built for them stay on the shared bill.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a shared house where one housemate brings in a hot tub. The landlord charges that room more for each unit of electricity, and a small fine if it books a bigger fuse than it uses.</p>
<p>The new cable and fuse box still go on the household bill, split between everyone. The fine covers a slice of an idle fuse, not the box itself.</p>
</details>

## How the bill is split

The Energy Commission, Malaysia's regulator, sets the charges of Tenaga Nasional, the company that owns the grid in the peninsula, for periods of about three years.

A large user's bill has separate lines. The energy charge pays for each unit used. The capacity and network charges pay for power stations and wires, by the kilowatt of maximum demand, the most power drawn at once.

The class Tenaga applies to data centres supplied above 50 kilovolts, called high voltage ultra, raises only the energy line. Its capacity and network charges match ordinary high voltage users exactly.

The second tool is the connected load charge. A new large customer that uses less than 85 per cent of the demand it declared, phase by phase, for four years pays a penalty on the shortfall. That penalty is about a fifth of what the capacity and network lines would charge for the same kilowatt.

Supply cannot simply wait for load. A substation and its lines take years to build, so the wires go in before the data centre fills them.

## Where the wires end up

Tenaga's grid allowance for 2025 to 2027 includes RM16.3 billion of contingent spending, released when set triggers are met. Its own investor deck says this partly meets demand from data centres with signed supply agreements. It earns the same regulated return as the rest of the grid and is recovered through the shared tariff.

The wires are also running ahead of use. In June 2026 Tenaga's data centre customers drew 1.26 gigawatts, against 5.65 gigawatts of maximum demand connected for them.

## What would prove me wrong

A new regulatory period starts on 1 January 2028, and ministers say data centres should bear the grid costs they cause.

I am wrong if that tariff gives data centres capacity or network charges above ordinary high voltage users, or bills the wires built for each campus to its developer. Either would take the wires off the shared bill.
