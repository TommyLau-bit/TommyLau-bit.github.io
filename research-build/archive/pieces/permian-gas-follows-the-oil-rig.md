Most of the gas produced in the Permian Basin was never drilled for. It comes up oil wells in West Texas and New Mexico, mixed in with the crude.

That makes the Permian an odd fuel supplier. Its buyers, including Texas power plants and the data centres they serve, have almost no say in how much gas exists.

Oil drillers decide that. Pipelines decide how much of it gets out.

In the Permian, gas follows the oil well, not the data centre.

## How Permian gas is made

Associated gas is gas that flows out of an oil well alongside the crude. Most Permian gas is made this way, says the US Energy Information Administration, EIA. And the basin's wells now bring up more gas with every barrel of oil than they used to.

Shale wells fade fast. By EIA's figures, Permian wells already flowing lose roughly a third of the basin's gas output a year. Only new wells replace it, and they are drilled for oil.

Then the gas has to leave, by pipeline to the Gulf Coast and to Texas power plants, [some built beside data centres](/journal/the-way-out-has-its-own-queue/). When the pipes are full, it has nowhere to go. Beyond a short window, Texas lets producers burn off casinghead gas, its legal name for gas from oil wells, only with an exception from the Railroad Commission. A missing pipeline is one accepted reason.

Supply cannot simply chase a new buyer. Nobody drills a Permian oil well for its gas, and a big pipeline takes years. Matterhorn Express, which carries gas from the Waha hub in West Texas towards Houston, opened in October 2024, nearly two and a half years after its builders committed to it.

In 2024, the year Matterhorn opened, gas at Waha traded below zero on 42 per cent of trading days. EIA puts that down to Permian output outrunning the pipelines.

## What the Permian did when the rigs left

In the year to early 2026, the number of rigs drilling in the Permian fell by a fifth. Over the same year the basin's gas output rose by about a twelfth.

Fewer rigs did not mean fewer oil wells. By EIA's count each remaining rig drilled more wells than a year before, and each barrel of oil brought more gas with it.

## What would prove me wrong

If oil drillers set the supply, power buyers should not be pulling new Permian drilling. I am wrong if, by the end of 2027, Baker Hughes' weekly count shows more than one in ten Permian rigs drilling for gas rather than oil.

I am also wrong if, before then, a Permian producer says it is adding rigs or wells to meet gas contracts with power plants or data centres.
