CAISO, which runs most of California's grid, publishes where its power comes from every five minutes. I counted this summer's evenings. On 66 of 122, batteries were the largest single source at the evening peak. In 2023 they led on none.

On the twelve hardest evenings, they led on none again. Gas did.

That matters for a claim of mine. In September I argued that grid batteries are [increasingly bought as connection equipment](/journal/what-the-battery-is-really-for/), not to trade power. California's 17 gigawatt fleet is the honest case against it.

California's batteries still shift the sun, but the hardest evenings outgrow them.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a hot water tank heated cheaply at midday. On an ordinary evening it covers the family's showers and the boiler barely fires.</p>
<p>When relatives come to stay, the tank gives exactly what it always gives. The extra showers fall to the boiler, because nobody fits a bigger tank for one busy week.</p>
</details>

## How the evening works

Solar floods the grid at midday, then fades as people get home. What the rest of the system must cover is the net peak, which is demand minus wind and solar. In California it arrives around eight in the evening.

Most of CAISO's batteries can run at full power for four hours, then need recharging. They fill up on midday sun and empty into that peak. CAISO's market monitor finds its batteries earned about four times as much last year from shifting energy as from regulation, the second by second balancing service. That is a time-shifter.

A heatwave makes the peak taller, but it does not make the fleet bigger. On the hardest evenings the batteries delivered about as much as on ordinary ones, and the extra demand fell to gas. Supply cannot simply catch up, because CAISO's fleet grew by only about 2 gigawatts in the past year.

## What the summer showed

On the twelve evenings of 2026 with the highest net load, batteries supplied about 11 gigawatts at the peak, a little above their summer average. Gas still supplied between 3 and 12 gigawatts more.

PJM, the grid from Chicago to Virginia, shows the same battery against a different calendar of risk. In March 2025 PJM estimated a four-hour battery was worth 94 per cent of its size against summer risk, and 43 per cent against winter risk. Its risk is shifting towards winter, and PJM names data centres as the main reason.

On this evidence my September claim fits, at most, the newest buyers beside data centres. It does not describe California's fleet, so I am narrowing it.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if, from June to September 2027, batteries lead CAISO's net peak on two or more of the twelve hardest evenings. This year the smallest gap was under 3 gigawatts, so one more year of new batteries could close it.

I am also wrong if PJM's rating for a four-hour battery holds at 57 per cent or more in its next auction, instead of falling as PJM's own outlook expects.
