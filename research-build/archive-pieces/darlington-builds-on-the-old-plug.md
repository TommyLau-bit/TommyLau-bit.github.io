Sometime in 2028, Ontario Power Generation, the province's public power company, expects to ask for approval to build three more small reactors at Darlington, east of Toronto.

The budget it attaches will answer a question no Western project has yet answered: does the second small reactor cost less than the first?

The first is already under construction there, the first small modular reactor being built in the G7. All four are identical, on one site that has run nuclear plant for decades.

Darlington's old site gets the first reactor built. The second reactor is the real test.

## How Darlington works

Each unit is a BWRX-300, a 300 megawatt boiling water reactor from GE Vernova Hitachi. In a boiling water reactor, the water cooling the fuel boils, and its steam drives the turbine directly.

The promise of a small reactor is learning. The first unit carries the costs of a first build: a finished design, a regulator reviewing it for the first time, suppliers qualifying parts. Later identical units should not pay those again.

Darlington gives that learning a fair chance. Its existing station has four large reactors, which OPG finished rebuilding in early 2026, so crews and suppliers are on hand.

The grid link is new but short: Hydro One, the provincial grid owner, plans a 22 kilometre line inside an existing corridor, beside lines already there. That is a far smaller job than the hundreds of miles of new lines that would have held up [Constellation's restart](/journal/constellation-restarts-the-reactor/) had it not borrowed a connection.

Ontario wants the power. Its grid operator, the IESO, forecasts demand rising 65 per cent by 2050, with data centres making up 8.6 per cent of all demand by then.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Few places hold a licensed site, a grid corridor and an experienced nuclear crew at once.

## What Darlington has done so far

The safety regulator, the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, licensed construction in April 2025. The reactor building's foundation was lowered into its shaft in April 2026, and OPG has applied for a licence to operate.

The date has already moved once. GE Vernova spoke of commercial operation by the end of 2029. OPG now targets the grid a year later.

Ontario put the first unit at C$6.1 billion, with shared systems costed separately, and says costs should fall with each later unit. Unlike [Oklo](/journal/oklo-sells-the-electricity/), the builder is public: OPG owns most of it, federal and provincial funds hold minority stakes, and Ontario's power customers stand behind it.

## What would prove me wrong

The 2025 budget already leaves room for three cheaper units. Take out the first unit and the shared systems, and about C$13.2 billion remains for the other three.

I am wrong about repetition if the approval OPG seeks puts those three together above that amount. I am wrong about the old site if the first unit has not sent power to the grid by the end of 2030.
