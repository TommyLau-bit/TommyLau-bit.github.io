From January 2027, a new data centre connecting to Dominion Energy's grid in Virginia signs up for 14 years. It pays a minimum for the grid it books, whether it switches on or not.

That rule comes with GS-5, Virginia's new rate class for customers of 25 megawatts or more. It is widely read as data centres finally paying for their wires.

Read the rule closely. It guards against an empty campus, not a full one.

Virginia's new class insures households against the data centre that never arrives, not the one that does.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a street where a newcomer orders a far bigger water supply. The water company makes them pay for the pipe from the road to their door, and promise to pay for much of the supply they booked for years, taps on or off.</p>
<p>That protects the neighbours if the newcomer never moves in. The wider main dug down the whole street is still split across every house's bill, and it lasts far longer than the promise.</p>
</details>

## How the floor works

Dominion splits its costs three ways. Generation is the power stations. Transmission is the high voltage lines that carry bulk power. Distribution is the substations and local wires.

Each class of customer pays a share of each, set largely by how much it draws when the grid is busiest.

Until now data centres have sat in a class shared with large factories and offices. There the substation built for one campus was already its own cost. Dominion's contracts require minimum payments that recover it in full.

The new class adds a bigger promise. The customer contracts its demand, the most power it may draw at once, and pays a minimum on it for the whole term, backed by collateral. The minimum covers generation, transmission and distribution.

That minimum is a floor, not a surcharge. A campus that fills its booking pays the class's normal rates, including its share of the shared lines.

Supply cannot wait for the load to prove itself. A new high voltage line takes years to approve and build, so Dominion builds before campuses fill. The floor makes sure someone pays if they never do.

## What the floor leaves out

Dominion's latest annual report sets out the rule, and its purpose: to limit subsidies between classes and stranded costs, meaning assets left unpaid for when a customer leaves. Data centres were already 28 per cent of its electricity sales in 2025.

Virginia's legislative audit office showed in 2024 what a floor cannot reach. Even with rates fairly allocated, data centre growth could add $14 to $37 a month to a typical household's power and lines costs by 2040.

That is growth that arrives, which a minimum does not touch. And new power stations and lines are paid off over 20 to 40 years, well past the contract.

## What would prove me wrong

In July 2026 Virginia's regulator told Dominion to propose up-front charges for the lines and substations that connect a single large customer. It also asked whether costs further up the network could go to the new class.

I am wrong if, by the end of 2027, the class is made to pay directly for shared network lines, such as the planned 765 kilovolt Valley Link, rather than through their share of demand.
