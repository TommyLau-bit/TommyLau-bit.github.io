Johor is not short of rivers. Singapore may draw roughly 1,100 megalitres of raw water a day from the Johor River. A megalitre is a million litres.

Then Singapore sells some of it back. In 2017 Singapore said it regularly supplied Johor with roughly 70 megalitres of treated water a day.

A state that exports raw water and imports clean water is not running dry. It is short of the plants that clean it, and Ranhill SAJ, its water operator, cannot build them as fast as data centres arrive.

Johor's water limit is not the river. It is the treatment plant.

## How clean water gets made

River water is not what reaches a tap or a cooling tower. It must first pass through a treatment plant, which settles, filters and disinfects it. In Johor that is done by Ranhill SAJ, overseen by the state regulator BAKAJ and the national regulator SPAN.

The buffer that matters is the reserve margin, the spare treatment capacity above what customers draw on an average day. SPAN recommends at least 15%, because plants break down, rivers get polluted and dry spells cut output.

Data centres lean on that buffer because their cooling towers evaporate clean water. Dirty river water would clog the tower.

Here the bottleneck is the building, not the rain. Raw water can usually be found, but a plant needs land, an intake, mains and years of construction. So the queue for plants sets the pace in Johor.

That queue shapes how Johor's campuses are cooled and which districts can host them. It does not decide whether they come, which is the [wider point about data centre water](/journal/water-is-a-siting-problem/).

## What Johor's own figures show

Johor's state assembly heard a year ago that demand will reach about 2,700 megalitres a day by 2030. Its plants were built to make 2,352.

This June Malaysia's water minister put Johor's reserve margin at about 14.4%, still under SPAN's line. Federal projects due by 2027 add 1,337 megalitres a day, mostly dams, barrages and raw water pipes. Raw water is arriving first.

The treatment side is smaller. Johor's two plants under construction add 210 megalitres a day, and four more financed by the federal agency PAAB are under way.

The state's own rules confirm the squeeze. Johor stopped approving the lowest grades of data centre last year. In March its Mentri Besar repeated that the top grades are approved only if cooled by air, and none with high water demand. About 12 megalitres a day of recycled sewage water already cools campuses there.

## What would prove me wrong

If the six plants now under way all run on time, the margin should clear 15%. My bet is that they will not keep pace.

I am wrong if SPAN or the federal water ministry states Johor's reserve margin at 15% or more for the end of 2027. I am also wrong if Johor drops its air-cooling rule before then because clean water has caught up, or if its next major cuts are officially attributed to drought or raw water shortage.
