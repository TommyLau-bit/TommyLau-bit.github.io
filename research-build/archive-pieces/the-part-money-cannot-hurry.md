Malaysia's grid operator did something unusual last year. It cut the time needed to connect a new project to the grid from thirty six months to twelve.

That was a genuine piece of administration, and it worked. It is also the most useful experiment anyone has run on the AI buildout, because of what it could not do.

It could not make a transformer any faster.

A connection queue looks like one line. It is really two, and only one of them answers to a government.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Getting a new kitchen fitted involves admin: the council signing off, the installer finding a slot, paperwork moving between desks. Push hard enough and that part of the wait shrinks.</p>
<p>The rest of the wait is the stone worktop, cut to measure in a workshop with a full order book. No amount of chasing makes it arrive sooner. A grid connection is the same shape: half desks, half a machine the size of a small house, built to order.</p>
</details>

## Two clocks inside one queue

A developer waiting for a grid connection is waiting for two things at once.

The first is permission and position: studies, approvals, a place in the connection order, and agreement on who pays. That is process, run on schedules a government can rewrite.

The second is plant. A substation holds switchgear, the heavy equipment that makes and breaks high voltage connections safely, and at its centre a large power transformer. The transformer steps voltage down from the level used for long distance transport to one a site can use.

A large power transformer is not a catalogue product. It is designed for its site, wound from a great deal of copper around a core of grain oriented electrical steel, a specialist material made by a few mills worldwide. Then it is tested, because a unit that fails takes the site with it. None of those steps is inefficiency.

A policy variable can be compressed by decision. A manufacturing variable can only be compressed by building more factories, which itself takes years.

## What Malaysia's own scheme shows

Tenaga Nasional, the national utility, created the Green Lane Pathway behind the cut to twelve months. Thirty three projects went through it by March 2026.

You do not build a programme to compress connection time unless connection, rather than generation, was stopping projects. In Johor, Wood Mackenzie notes, generation capacity remains sufficient. Access to the wires decides whether a project proceeds.

So the first clock moved a long way. Nothing in the scheme could move the second.

**Policy compression has a floor, and the floor is made of steel and copper.**

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if connection timelines keep compressing across the board the way the Green Lane did. Then a place near the front of the queue stops mattering, and this is a two year delay rather than a lasting one.

I am also wrong if transformer and high voltage cable lead times normalise faster than utility spending is committed. And I am wrong if power built on site removes the constraint rather than moving it, the part of my argument most likely to fail.
