An AI data centre cannot switch on without two kinds of heavy electrical gear. A transformer at the grid connection steps the voltage down. Switchgear inside the site's fence routes the power and cuts off faults.

Four companies this site follows make much of it: Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, Eaton and Schneider Electric. Each is building new factories.

Each also reports a bigger pile of signed orders than it had a year ago.

The makers are building fast enough to keep the queue, not to clear it.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>A popular hairdresser is booked weeks ahead. She adds a second chair, but only once the bookings justify it, and the diary still runs weeks ahead.</p>
<p>Counting her chairs tells you she is growing. Counting the weeks in her diary tells you whether you will get a cut sooner. For grid equipment, the diary runs in years.</p>
</details>

## How it works

A large power transformer is designed for its site, wound around a core of specialist steel and tested before it ships. Switchgear, and the electrical rooms that hold it, is simpler to make. But data centres now order it in quantities the factories were not built for.

Supply cannot simply catch up, for three reasons. A new factory takes two to three years from announcement to its first product. Makers add floor space only once signed orders justify it, because a plant runs for decades and the industry remembers its empty years. And finished gear must still be installed and tested on site by electricians, who are scarce too.

So the queue is best measured in time. Take the orders a maker has signed but not yet delivered, its backlog, and divide by a year of its sales. The answer is how long its factories would take to clear the pile if no new order came in.

That is a proxy for the wait, not a quoted delivery time. Higher prices and earlier bookings can lengthen it as well.

## The number that matters

Hitachi Energy, by its own estimate the maker of one in six of the world's installed transformers, had about three years of work waiting at the end of September 2026. Its parent plans to keep it about that long through 2030, while it builds or expands more than 40 factories.

Siemens Energy's grid business has about four years waiting, though that includes long cross-country power links. Eaton's electrical business in the Americas, which makes the gear inside the fence, has about a year, up for five quarters running.

Siemens Energy has also estimated the gap for the whole industry. In Europe and North America, demand for large transformers ran about 40% ahead of every maker's capacity in 2025. In 2030, after years of factory building, it is still about 10% ahead.

A new factory adds to output. It does not shorten the diary.

## The mechanism in depth

Three of the four makers publish a backlog for the business that sells this gear; Schneider reports only a "record backlog". Dividing backlog by the same business's revenue over the previous four quarters gives years of work, my arithmetic from each latest filing.

| Maker, business | Backlog | Revenue, previous four quarters | Years of work | Earlier point | Orders against revenue | Latest filing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hitachi Energy | $66.9bn, 30 September 2026 | about $21.6bn | about 3.1 | about 2.8 (31 March 2025) | 1.66 times, fiscal 2025 | Hitachi results, 28 October 2026 |
| Siemens Energy, Grid Technologies | €51bn, 30 June 2026 | €12.9bn | about 4.0 | 3.56 (30 September 2024) | 1.88 times, nine months to June 2026 | Earnings release, 5 August 2026 |
| Eaton, Electrical Americas | $15.2bn, 30 June 2026 | $14.5bn | 1.05, about 12.6 months | 0.93 (30 June 2025) | book-to-bill 1.3, twelve months | Form 10-Q, 31 July 2026 |

The ratios are not like for like. The two grid makers carry HVDC links, high voltage direct current lines that take years to build. Eaton's gear mostly ships within a year. Direction matters, not comparison.

The ratio rises when prices go up before deliveries catch up, when buyers book earlier for later delivery, and, for Hitachi's dollar figures, when the euro strengthens. None of the three splits backlog into price and volume. It can also fall while the pile grows, as the test below shows.

<a id="hitachi-energy-plans-the-queue"></a>
## Hitachi Energy: a queue kept at three years

Hitachi Energy, Hitachi's power grids business, makes large power transformers, HVDC links and high voltage switchgear. By Hitachi's estimate it has installed one in six of the world's transformers.

Its backlog was $57.9bn at 31 March 2026, 2.93 years of work, and $63.6bn at 30 June, 3.06. On 28 October 2026 Hitachi reported $66.9bn at 30 September, up 16% since March, about 3.1 years. Hitachi credited the quarter's orders to large HVDC awards in Europe and to data centres.

The plan keeps the wait. At its investor day on 10 June 2026, Hitachi showed backlog rising from about $60bn in fiscal 2025 to about $100bn in fiscal 2030, and revenue from $20bn to $36bn, with backlog "expected to remain stable at 2.5-3x" revenue. The plan figures end near 2.8, but the $100bn includes framework agreements and capacity reservations, Andreas Schierenbeck said, so it is not on the basis of reported backlog. Hitachi Energy is "not building over-capacity" (Hitachi's translated summary of the session).

One correction to my October piece: South Boston "should operate by 2028" came from a trade press quote. Hitachi's own release gives no date. Gallman's 2029 start is Hitachi Energy's own.

<a id="siemens-energy-measures-the-shortage"></a>
## Siemens Energy: the chart for the whole industry

At its capital market day on 20 November 2025, Siemens Energy set demand for large and medium power transformers in Europe and North America against the capacity of all market players there. It measured both in GVA, gigavolt-amperes, the unit for how much power a transformer carries. The gap was about 40% in fiscal 2025 and about 10% in fiscal 2030. Only those labels are its own; by my reading of the bars, capacity nearly doubles from fiscal 2023.

Read as yearly flows, each year's shortfall joins the pile. That reading is mine. If the chart counts only factories in the region, imports fill part of the gap.

| Quarter end | Grid backlog | Years of work |
|---|---|---|
| 30 September 2024 | €33bn | 3.56 |
| 30 September 2025 | €42bn | 3.72 |
| 31 March 2026 | €49bn | 4.05 |
| 30 June 2026 | €51bn | 3.96 |

The backlog is rounded to €1bn, worth about 0.04 on each ratio. It is global, and HVDC and offshore links, with five to seven years of reach, are two thirds of its €24bn systems backlog.

Siemens Energy spent about €600m in fiscal 2023 to 2025, adding about 20% capacity, and is investing over €2bn more in transformer and switchgear capacity by fiscal 2028. Nuremberg grows by about half by 2028. Its release of 14 February 2024 expected Charlotte's first transformers in early 2026. My October piece said it had slipped a year; that rested on a press report, and no Siemens Energy release I found, including that of 3 February 2026, gives a new date.

<a id="eaton-order-book-outruns-it"></a>
## Eaton: the queue inside the fence

Eaton's Electrical Americas business makes the switchgear, UPS systems and electrical rooms between the grid connection and the racks. A UPS, uninterruptible power supply, is a large battery that carries the load through a dip in grid power.

| Quarter end | Backlog | Years of work |
|---|---|---|
| 31 December 2024 | $10,141m | 0.89 |
| 30 June 2025 | $11,377m | 0.93 |
| 31 December 2025 | $13,246m | 1.00 |
| 30 June 2026 | $15,175m | 1.05 |

In the second quarter of 2026 the segment sold $3,951m, up 18% organic, which strips out acquisitions and currency. Twelve-month orders rose 41% organic and backlog 33% (release and Form 10-Q, 31 July 2026). The latest rise was only 0.01.

The queue had a cost: segment margin fell from 29.5% to 27.5% year on year, with 470 basis points, hundredths of a percentage point, from commodity inflation. Orders priced months ago met higher metal costs.

The transformer and switchgear plants arrive in 2027. Call quotes in my October piece came from a third-party transcript and are not repeated.

<a id="schneider-sells-the-finished-system"></a>
## Schneider Electric: selling the finished system

Schneider gives no backlog figure. Its evidence is what customers buy. Products are catalogue parts fitted on site; Systems include prefabricated power and cooling rooms built and tested in a factory, and large UPS.

| Quarter | Systems, organic growth | Products, organic growth | Systems share of revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 2025 | 19% | 4% | not given |
| Q1 2026 | 16% | 9% | 33% |
| Q2 2026 | 28% | 13% | 35% |
| Q3 2026 | 22% | 17% | 35% |

Systems was 31% of revenue in 2024 and 34% in 2025. On 16 October 2026 Schneider reported third quarter revenue of €11,460m, up 16.9% organic, with data centre sales "up strong double-digit", "notably in prefabricated solutions".

The mechanism I argued is site labour. Every panel must be installed and commissioned, tested under power, before it carries load. Frédéric Godemel, who runs Schneider's energy business, described "tension on the number of people to commission product and install them" (capital markets day, 11 December 2025). Prefabrication moves part of that work into the factory, and the half year results say the mix "adversely impacted" gross margin.

The third quarter moves against me. Products grew 17%, "majority volume-driven", the gap with Systems narrowing from 15 points to five. More catalogue parts by volume is the opposite of my mechanism. Systems still grew faster, but from rounded shares it was about 34% of nine-month revenue, somewhere from 33.9% to 34.9%, on my test's line.

## Claims on record

Each claim is quoted word for word from the claims page, with the date its piece went live.

**Schneider Electric sells AI data centres the finished system, not the parts** (7 October 2026)

Claim: "Schneider Electric's AI data centre growth is arriving as finished systems, built and tested in its factories, rather than as loose parts, because skilled hours on site are now scarcer than equipment, so Systems will keep taking a larger share of its revenue."

Breaks if: "Systems falls below 34 per cent of Schneider's group revenue, its 2025 level, for the full year 2026 or 2027 while Schneider describes Data Centre and Networks sales as growing double digit; or Products outgrows Systems on an organic basis for two quarters running while data centres remain Schneider's main growth driver."

Where later evidence cuts against it: not broken, but close. In the third quarter of 2026 Products grew 17% against 22% for Systems, and Schneider says Products growth was "majority volume-driven": more loose parts, not just higher prices. The nine-month Systems share is about 34% from rounded shares (33.9% to 34.9%), on the line. The share test checks the outcome, not the cause: buyers may want prefabrication for speed as much as for scarce electricians.

**Hitachi Energy is spending more than $9 billion on factories, and still plans a three-year queue** (22 October 2026)

Claim: "Hitachi Energy is adding factories only as fast as signed orders justify, and its order backlog will stay at or above about 2.5 times annual revenue, roughly three years of work, through fiscal 2030, which ends on 31 March 2031, so the queue for its transformers and HVDC links will not clear."

Breaks if: "Hitachi Energy's order backlog divided by its revenue over the previous four quarters, both in US dollars as Hitachi reports them (about 3.06 at 30 June 2026), falls below 2.5 at two consecutive quarter ends before 31 March 2031, the end of fiscal 2030; or its orders for any full fiscal year up to fiscal 2030 fall below its revenue for that year."

Since the piece: the ratio rose to about 3.1 at 30 September 2026 (Hitachi, 28 October 2026). The claim holds. Its South Boston date came from trade press, not Hitachi.

**Eaton is shipping more electrical gear than ever, and its order book is growing faster still** (26 October 2026)

Claim: "Eaton is shipping more electrical gear than ever, yet its Electrical Americas backlog keeps growing faster than its sales, so the queue for the equipment inside a data centre's fence, measured in months of sales on order, will keep lengthening until its new plants arrive in 2027."

Breaks if: "Electrical Americas backlog divided by the segment's sales over the previous four quarters (1.05 at June 2026, up from 1.04 in March) falls for two consecutive quarters before mid 2027, when the new plants start; or falls below 0.93, its June 2025 level, before the end of 2027; or the segment's twelve-month book-to-bill falls below 1.0 for two quarters running; or Electrical Americas margin is still falling year on year after Eaton reports price and cost back to neutral."

Since the piece: no new figures by 30 October 2026. The rise slowed to 0.01, and some of it may be price.

**Siemens Energy has measured the transformer shortage, and its own chart runs to 2030** (28 October 2026)

Claim: "By Siemens Energy's own estimate, demand for large and medium power transformers in Europe and North America exceeds the combined capacity of all market players in the region in every year through its fiscal 2030, which ends on 30 September 2030, so unfilled orders keep accumulating even as the gap narrows: Grid Technologies' orders stay above its revenue each fiscal year and its backlog in euros keeps growing."

Breaks if: "Before 30 September 2030: Grid Technologies' orders fall below its revenue for any full fiscal year; or its order backlog in euros, as Siemens Energy reports it (€51bn at 30 June 2026), falls for two consecutive quarters; or Siemens Energy publishes a revised estimate showing transformer capacity meeting demand in Europe and North America before fiscal 2030."

Since the piece: no new figures by 30 October 2026. Its Charlotte slip rested on a press report; Siemens Energy's last stated date is early 2026, and its release of 3 February 2026 gives none.

**New: the equipment queue is kept, not cleared** (dated when this piece goes live)

Claim: "The first wave of new transformer and switchgear plants will not shorten the equipment queue in years of work: at 30 June 2028, Siemens Energy Grid Technologies' order backlog will still be at least 3.6 times its revenue over the previous four quarters (3.96 at 30 June 2026), and Eaton Electrical Americas' backlog at least 1.0 times its sales over the previous four quarters (1.05), even though Siemens Energy's own chart allows its ratio to fall while the pile grows."

Breaks if: "At 30 June 2028, as each company reports it, Siemens Energy Grid Technologies' order backlog in euros divided by its revenue over the previous four quarters is below 3.6, or Eaton Electrical Americas' backlog divided by its sales over the previous four quarters is below 1.0."

## What would prove me wrong

What is new is the Siemens Energy test. Its October claim tests only the pile in euros; this one tests the wait in years, which its own chart lets fall. Eaton's limb runs a year past its 2027 plants, at 1.0 rather than its old 0.93 floor. Hitachi is left out because its own claim already tests its ratio to 2031.

It is a real risk. Orders 10% above output with output growing 6% a year take Siemens Energy's 3.96 to about 3.7 in two years, close to the line. Eaton's ratio rose only 0.01 last quarter, and Jonesville and Bellevue should be running before mid 2028.

A pass would not prove a longer wait, since price, early bookings, currency and HVDC contracts can hold a ratio up. Schneider publishes no backlog.
