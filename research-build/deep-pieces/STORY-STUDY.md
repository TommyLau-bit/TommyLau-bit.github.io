# Story study: how well-told industrial pieces carry one argument

Written 30 October 2026 for the deep-piece rewrites. The problem it answers: the equipment queue piece has the right facts but reads as four company notes stapled together, carries five claims, uses tables where a chart should go, and has no storyline.

## How this was read

Eight pieces, each read through a text fetch of the free page. Paywalled outlets were skipped and no block was bypassed: Bloomberg's transformer explainer (29 May 2025) returned 403; FT Big Reads and Economist briefings sit behind paywalls, and the only Economist copy found was an unverified mirror, so it is not used. SemiAnalysis is studied on its free portion only. Two of the eight (the Reuters wire and the POWER feature) are included partly as useful contrasts: they are competent, but they show the "company paragraph" habit the journal needs to drop.

---

## 1. How to Build an AI Data Center

- **Outlet, author, date, URL:** Construction Physics (Brian Potter, with the Institute for Progress), 10 June 2024. https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-to-build-an-ai-data-center
- **Spine:** As AI makes data centres bigger and denser, electricity, not chips, becomes the binding constraint on the buildout.
- **Opening:** Three short paragraphs move from a paradox to the stakes. Software feels weightless; but it runs on hardware, and a frontier model's training would take a phone tens of thousands of years; so it was trained in a building, and those buildings are now going up everywhere. The reader is led from an intuition they hold to the physical object the piece is about, in about 120 words.
- **How it moves:** Five beats. Stakes (money pouring in, power already the complaint). The object explained from first principles (racks, aisles, cooling loops, redundancy), with everyday comparisons for every unit. The trend (racks from 1 kW to tens of kW, sites from 10 MW to hundreds). The twist that AI brings (dense racks break air cooling). A conclusion that restates the constraint in scale terms. Companies and people (Meta, Amazon, Nvidia, a utility, an executive quote) arrive only as evidence inside a beat; none gets a section. Places (Virginia, Ireland, Singapore) are cases of the constraint, not tours.
- **Numbers and visuals:** Dense with numbers, but each is translated (a campus equals a small city; a rack equals so many hair dryers). Seven figures, no tables. Two are data charts: the compute trend (proves "GPT-4 was part of a long trend") and US data centre electricity use flattening in 2000 to 2014 (proves that efficiency once held demand down, which sets up the question of whether it still can). Five are diagrams that teach the object. Each sits directly under the sentence it illustrates.
- **Uncertainty:** Gives the sceptics' case a fair hearing (past efficiency gains, better chips, bad extrapolation), concedes what is right in it, then explains why it probably fails now. Flags vendor numbers as marketing. Lands deliberately in the middle on aggregate demand.
- **Ending:** No new evidence. Restates the spine at the largest scale (a campus the size of a nuclear plant) and says the constraint tightens from here.

## 2. How ASML Got EUV

- **Outlet, author, date, URL:** Construction Physics (Brian Potter), 20 November 2025. https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-asml-got-euv
- **Spine:** American research built EUV lithography, but American firms had left the lithography market, so one Dutch company was left to commercialise it.
- **Opening:** Paragraph one sets the domain (chip progress rides on lithography). Paragraph two plants a paradox (one Dutch firm makes the machine, yet the research was largely American). Paragraph three asks the question outright: so why ASML? The whole piece answers that one question.
- **How it moves:** Chronological, but every era is a step in the answer. How lithography works and why wavelength is the limit. The decades of failed alternatives, and optical lithography refusing to die. The birth of EUV against sceptical reviewers. The Intel-led consortium that let ASML in while US toolmakers had already dropped out. Conclusion. Companies are actors in a plot (Intel bets, Nikon and Canon fall away, ASML is admitted), not profiles. One person (an early Japanese researcher) carries the human thread.
- **Numbers and visuals:** About 80 numbers, mostly as a running series: wavelength shrinking from 436 nm to 13.5 nm, and Intel's bets growing in size. Only two figures: a photograph of an early tool, and one chart of lens improvement over time that proves a single sentence, that optical lithography kept beating predictions of its own end. No tables.
- **Uncertainty:** Shows the doubt as part of the story (reviewers who thought it could not work; a task force that ranked EUV last of four options). Puts definitional quibbles in footnotes so they do not break the line.
- **Ending:** Answers the opening question in one move: building a technology and winning its market are different achievements. The question asked in paragraph three is closed in the last paragraph.

## 3. How the world's first electric grid was built

- **Outlet, author, date, URL:** Works in Progress (Alex Chalmers), 19 March 2026. https://worksinprogress.co/issue/how-the-worlds-first-electric-grid-was-built/
- **Spine:** Britain got a national grid only when fragmented local operators were made to connect, and every time that pressure eased, fragmentation cost money again.
- **Opening:** A single scene: a Victorian gallery owner installs a generator to light his paintings and sells the spare power to neighbours. It grows into a power station, then stumbles through fires, overruns and a fatal accident. Only then does the writer widen: this was typical. The anecdote is the argument in miniature.
- **How it moves:** A short technical detour (why alternating current makes interconnection possible) gives the reader the one idea needed to follow the rest. Then a long chronological section in which each episode (the 1920s inquiry, the 1930s grid, nationalisation, the supergrid, privatisation, the 2024 reorganisation) is evidence for the same claim about coordination. Institutions appear as proof of an incentive problem, never as their own subject.
- **Numbers and visuals:** Roughly 70 numbers, half of them dates. The memorable ones count the mess (dozens of systems, dozens of voltages, hundreds of stations). One data chart, long-run prices, proves that prices fell steadily and then more slowly; the rest are photographs that anchor scenes.
- **Uncertainty:** States a popular critique (private owners favour dividends), calls it too simple, and answers it with a cost figure. Treats nationalisation's flaws with the same even hand.
- **Ending:** Brings the story to the present and shows it rhyming with the 1920s: control has been re-centralised while ownership stays private. The ending returns to the opening problem, fragmentation.

## 4. Why did renewables become so cheap so fast?

- **Outlet, author, date, URL:** Our World in Data (Max Roser), 1 December 2020, charts updated 2025. https://ourworldindata.org/cheap-renewables-growth
- **Spine:** Renewables got cheaper than fossil power because their costs fall along learning curves that fossil fuels do not have, so scaling them now is the fastest route to cheap clean power.
- **Opening:** Paragraph one states the condition (clean power must be cheaper to win). Paragraph two states the reversal (it now is, in most places). Paragraph three names the mechanism (learning curves) and its consequence (the gap will widen). Claim, reversal and mechanism are all on the table before the first chart.
- **How it moves:** Each section heading is a question or a step in the proof: what fossil power costs; what renewable power costs; why (learning curves); is it causation (a wartime natural experiment); does it hold for whole electricity prices; do fossil fuels and nuclear behave the same (no, and here is why); so what follows for policy. Technologies enter as tests of the mechanism: nuclear and coal are the control group, batteries a confirmation.
- **Numbers and visuals:** Seven data charts and one diagram. This is the cleanest chart discipline in the set: almost every chart has a sentence directly above it that says what the chart shows, and the chart proves only that sentence (for example, fossil electricity got cheaper over 15 years; renewables fell faster and crossed below it; module prices fall with cumulative output). Numbers are translated into human scale (a 1956 panel price, rent falling from one sum to another).
- **Uncertainty:** Concedes the strongest objection directly (cost per unit ignores storage and backup), handles reverse causation with evidence rather than assertion, gives ranges across studies, and is plain that learning does not take prices to zero.
- **Ending:** Restates the mechanism, then widens to stakes (emissions, health, incomes). No new evidence.

## 5. Can AI scaling continue through 2030?

- **Outlet, author, date, URL:** Epoch AI (Jaime Sevilla and colleagues), 20 August 2024. https://epoch.ai/blog/can-ai-scaling-continue-through-2030
- **Spine:** Training runs about 10,000 times today's are feasible by 2030, with power likely the first constraint to bind and chips the second.
- **Opening:** Growth of about four times a year is stated, compared with other famous fast build-outs, then turned into a question with four named candidate limits. The reader knows the structure of the whole report by paragraph three.
- **How it moves:** One section per constraint, each run as the same test: how much is there, how far does it stretch, what is the largest run it allows. The power section is a chain of arithmetic in about eleven beats, from one chip's draw to a cluster, to a campus, to the national grid, with each company deal (a nuclear contract, a reported campus plan) entering as a data point in the chain. A final section ranks the constraints against each other.
- **Numbers and visuals:** Well over a hundred numbers in the power section alone, but every one is a step in a calculation. Charts are summary devices: one chart puts all four constraints on a single axis so they can be compared; parameter tables carry low, middle and high inputs so the text does not have to.
- **Uncertainty:** The strongest in the set. Every estimate is a range with stated confidence, the writers say which inputs are rough guesses, and they flag where an estimate may be too optimistic and where too conservative.
- **Ending:** Restates the answer, sizes the stakes (possibly the largest technology project in history), and names which uncertainties remain largest and will be studied next.
- **Lesson for the journal:** this is the model for a testable claim. It is not the model for narrative; a reader who wants a story gets a report.

## 6. Datacenter Anatomy Part 1: Electrical Systems (free portion)

- **Outlet, authors, date, URL:** SemiAnalysis (Jeremie Eliahou Ontiveros, Dylan Patel, Daniel Nishball), 14 October 2024; the winners-and-losers section is paywalled and was not read. https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/datacenter-anatomy-part-1-electrical
- **Spine:** Blackwell-class racks above 100 kW with liquid cooling force data centres off low-density designs, which reshapes the power supply chain and decides which vendors win.
- **Opening:** Stakes (data centres are now critical infrastructure), then history (the industry drifted towards density with no standard), then the trigger (one Nvidia product makes density compulsory). A "why now" opening.
- **How it moves:** A walk along the power path from grid to chip, one piece of equipment per section, then a turn: why the old designs fail, proved by a case study of a company that had to scrap a building. Vendors appear mostly as sources of specifications; one company gets a section only because its failure is the proof.
- **Numbers and visuals:** About a hundred numbers and roughly thirty images, mostly diagrams and equipment photos. The two that argue are a capacity table (cluster size to megawatts) and a rack density chart that the writers use to attack an outside forecast.
- **Uncertainty:** Confident in tone. Trade-offs are stated (efficiency against transfer risk, battery fire risk), and estimates from permits are labelled as estimates.
- **Ending (free part):** Points forward to the paid winners-and-losers section.
- **Lesson for the journal:** the equipment walk works as a reference, not as a story. The single best move is the case study placed after the mechanism, as proof that the mechanism bites.

## 7. US power transformer buyers scramble for imports, factory slots

- **Outlet, date, URL:** Reuters wire, republished by Kitco, 11 May 2026; no individual byline shown. https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-05-11/us-power-transformer-buyers-scramble-imports-factory-slots
- **Spine:** Demand for transformers has outrun supply, so buyers pay premiums, import and reserve factory slots, and the gap will last for years.
- **Opening:** One-line news lead (shortages slow the grid build), one line defining a transformer for the general reader, one line of demand drivers with a single headline statistic. Claim, object, cause in three sentences.
- **How it moves:** Inverted pyramid in short beats: lead, definition, demand, the supply response conceded early, costs and delay, knock-on effects, imports, buyer behaviour (paying to reserve slots, refurbishing old units), the US factory list, the financing logic, the closing caveat. Companies appear in a run of announcement paragraphs, each attached to a sum and a date. This is the weakest stretch, and it is exactly the journal's habit in miniature.
- **Numbers and visuals:** About 14 figures beyond dates. No charts.
- **Uncertainty:** Carried by forecast hedges; no dissenting voice. All sources are consultants or makers.
- **Ending:** Repeats the spine as a forecast: new capacity will not close the gap within five years. The ending agrees with the lead, which is the one structural strength.

## 8. The Transformer Crisis: An Industry on the Brink

- **Outlet, author, date, URL:** POWER magazine (Sonal C. Patel), 26 June 2024. https://www.powermag.com/the-transformer-crisis-an-industry-on-the-brink/
- **Spine:** The shortage is structural (surging demand, an ageing fleet, a concentrated and customised maker base) and needs standardisation, capacity and shared long-term commitments to fix.
- **Opening:** Escalation in three steps: warnings for years, now a crisis; delivery in two to four years and prices up by most of their 2020 level; and the pre-pandemic baseline for contrast.
- **How it moves:** Topic sections (demand drivers, a basics sidebar, manufacturing, materials, standardisation, technology, growth). Executives are quoted at length and carry colour. The technology section turns into a product tour, one company per run-in head.
- **Numbers and visuals:** About a hundred numbers. One data chart, lead times rising from under a year to well over two, which is the piece's best single proof; the rest are a parts diagram and factory photographs. No tables.
- **Uncertainty:** Mostly borrowed hedges inside quotes; nearly all sources are industry insiders.
- **Ending:** Drifts to collaboration and shared risk, a softer point than the opening. The ending answers a different question from the one asked at the top.
- **Lesson for the journal:** a cautionary twin of the equipment queue piece. Strong facts, topic sections, a company tour, and an ending that does not close the opening.

## What the good ones share

1. **One question, answered at the end.** Potter on EUV asks "why ASML" in paragraph three and answers it in the final paragraph. Roser names the mechanism in paragraph three and every section tests it. The weaker pieces end on a different note from the one they opened on.
2. **Sections are steps in a proof, not topics.** Roser's headings read as questions in sequence; Epoch's sections run one identical test; Chalmers's eras are all evidence for one claim about coordination. POWER's sections are topics, and its reader has to build the argument.
3. **Companies are evidence, not chapters.** In every strong piece, a company appears because it proves a beat (Intel's bet, a scrapped building, a nuclear contract). The only company that gets its own section in the good pieces is one whose failure is the proof.
4. **One chart, one sentence.** The best chart discipline puts a plain sentence directly above each chart and lets the chart prove only that. Tables appear for parameters a reader may want to check, not for the argument.
5. **Few charts, chosen for the turn of the argument.** Potter's EUV piece has one chart. Chalmers has one. They chart the fact on which the argument turns.
6. **A running number.** EUV's wavelength, Epoch's four-times-a-year, Roser's falling price. One measure recurs and the reader learns to read it.
7. **Translation of every unit.** Potter's hair dryers and small cities; Roser's rent. The reader is never left with a bare unit.
8. **Counter-evidence gets its own beat, late, and is answered.** Potter's sceptics, Roser's system costs, Chalmers's privatisation critics. The objection is stated fairly, then met with a figure.
9. **Scene or paradox first, mechanism second, numbers third.** Lindsay's gallery; the weightless software; the Dutch monopoly on an American invention.

## STORY RULES

Each rule is checkable against a draft.

1. **One claim per piece.** The claim is one sentence, stated in plain words by the end of paragraph three. Anything that is a second claim (Schneider's prefab argument here) goes to its own piece or note.
2. **The opening is a paradox or a scene, not a definition.** Paragraph one must contain a tension a reader would want resolved (here: every maker is building factories, and every maker's queue is longer). Definitions come after the tension, never before.
3. **The last paragraph answers the question the first three paragraphs asked,** in different words and with no new evidence. Check: read paragraph three, then the last paragraph; they should read as question and answer.
4. **Every section heading is a step in the argument,** written as a statement or a question the reader is now asking (for example, "Why the makers will not overbuild"), never a company name or a topic label ("Eaton", "How it works").
5. **No company gets its own section.** A company appears only inside a beat, as evidence for that beat, in at most three sentences. If a company's paragraph could be cut and pasted into a different piece unchanged, it is a company note, not evidence.
6. **One running measure.** Choose one unit (here, years of work) and use it in every beat that carries a number. Other units appear only when converted into it or when they explain it.
7. **Every chart proves one sentence, and that sentence sits directly above it.** The chart caption repeats the claim, not the topic ("Every queue is longer than a year ago", not "Backlog to revenue, three makers").
8. **Two to four charts per deep piece, at the turns of the argument.** Chart the trend that sets up the problem, the gap that explains it, and the test that could break it. Nothing else earns a chart.
9. **Tables are for checkable inputs, not for the argument.** A table may appear only in a closing evidence section or as an expandable block; no table may sit in the storyline. If a table has a time column, it should be a chart.
10. **Numbers per paragraph: at most three, and each translated.** Every number in the storyline is followed by what it means for the wait, in words a non-specialist can repeat.
11. **Counter-evidence gets one named beat, two thirds of the way through,** stated in its strongest form and then answered with a figure or conceded with a limit. It is not scattered as hedges across every section.
12. **Mechanism before evidence.** The reader must know why the queue cannot clear (factory clock, makers' caution, site labour) before seeing any company figure that shows it has not cleared.
13. **Each beat opens with its point.** The first sentence of every section states what the section proves; the rest supports it. A reader skimming only first sentences should get the whole argument.
14. **Sourcing sits out of the line.** Dates of filings, release dates and arithmetic method go into a sources note, a footnote or a closing evidence block, not into the middle of a beat.
15. **Corrections and claim histories leave the storyline.** Notes such as "one correction to my October piece" belong in an update note or the claims page, not in the narrative.
16. **The investor layer extends the same spine.** Below the layer line, the reader gets the test, the exposure map and the evidence tables; it may add depth, but not a new claim.
17. **One analogy, used twice.** If the piece has an analogy (the hairdresser's diary), it appears once to explain the measure and returns once at the end; no second analogy.
18. **Length follows beats.** Six to nine beats, each 120 to 300 words in the storyline; if a beat needs more, it is two beats or it is a second piece.

## Beat outline: the equipment queue, rewritten

Spine: the makers of transformers and switchgear are building factories, but they build only as fast as orders justify, and orders keep outrunning them, so the wait for grid equipment will still be measured in years in 2030.

1. **The paradox (opening).** Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy and Eaton are all building plants, and all three have more years of work waiting than a year ago. State the spine by paragraph three. Hairdresser's diary as the one analogy.
2. **The measure.** The wait is best read as years of work: backlog divided by a year of sales. One paragraph on what it is and what it is not (a proxy, not a quoted delivery time). **Chart 1:** years of work over time for the three makers, three rising lines (Hitachi about 2.8 to about 3.1; Siemens Energy grid 3.56 to about 4.0; Eaton Electrical Americas 0.89 to 1.05). Sentence above it: every queue is longer than it was a year ago. Note under it that levels are not comparable (HVDC inflates the two grid makers), direction is.
3. **Why supply cannot jump: the factory clock.** A new plant takes two to three years from announcement to first output. **Chart 2:** a dot-and-line timeline of dated plants, announcement to due date (Charlotte, Jonesville, Gallman, Nuremberg, Bellevue). Sentence above it: most new floor space announced since 2024 lands from 2027 on. The full factory table moves to the evidence block.
4. **Why makers will not overbuild.** Makers add floor space only as signed orders justify, because plants run for decades and the industry remembers its empty years. Evidence: Hitachi's own plan keeps backlog at about 2.5 to 3 times revenue through 2030 while it builds more than 40 factories. Hitachi appears here as proof of intent, in three sentences.
5. **The size of the gap, by a maker's own count.** Siemens Energy's estimate: in Europe and North America, transformer demand ran about 40% ahead of all makers' capacity in 2025 and still about 10% ahead in 2030, even as capacity nearly doubles. **Chart 3:** demand against capacity, 2025 and 2030, with the gap labelled. Sentence above it: the gap narrows but never closes, and each year's shortfall joins the pile.
6. **The cost of the wait (optional, short).** Eaton's segment margin fell two points as orders priced earlier met higher metal costs: the queue is not free for the makers either. One paragraph, Eaton as evidence.
7. **What would shorten it (the counter-evidence beat).** The ratio can fall while the pile grows: orders 10% above output with output growing 6% a year take Siemens Energy from 3.96 to about 3.7 in two years. Eaton's rise slowed to 0.01. Price, early booking and currency can hold a ratio up without a longer real wait. State these at full strength, then say why the spine still holds.
8. **The test (end of storyline).** One claim, one date, one threshold: at 30 June 2028, Siemens Energy grid at or above 3.6 years and Eaton Electrical Americas at or above 1.0. Close by returning to the diary: the second chair has arrived, and the diary still runs years ahead. Below the layer line: exposure map, evidence tables (backlog, factories), sources.

**Schneider's prefab systems:** cut from this piece. It is a second claim with a different mechanism (scarce site labour, not factory capacity), its evidence is a revenue mix rather than a queue, and the third quarter of 2026 moved against it. It belongs in its own short note or update. At most, one sentence in beat 7 may mention that some buyers route around the queue by buying factory-built rooms, with a link.
