Hitachi Energy is building or expanding more than 40 factories to make the heavy electrical gear that AI data centres need. Yet it has told its investors that, as they open, it still expects two and a half to three years of orders to be waiting.

Siemens Energy and Eaton tell the same story. Each is building new plants, and each has a longer queue of unfilled orders than when it began announcing them.

That is not a failure to keep up. It is the plan. The makers are building fast enough to keep the queue, not to clear it, and on their own figures the wait for this gear will still be measured in years in 2030.

The gear is unglamorous. A transformer at the grid connection steps the voltage down. Switchgear inside the site's fence routes the power and cuts off faults. A data centre cannot switch on without both, and neither comes off a shelf. A large transformer is designed for its site, wound around a core of specialist steel and tested before it ships.

The wait cannot simply be bought away, for three reasons. A new factory takes two to three years from announcement to its first product. A plant runs for decades, and the industry remembers years when its halls stood half empty, so makers add floor space only once signed orders justify it. And every piece of gear must still be installed and tested on site by electricians, who are scarce too.

The clearest measure of the wait is in the makers' own order books. Take the orders a maker has signed but not yet delivered, and divide by a year of its sales. That gives the years its factories would need to clear the pile if no new order arrived. At Hitachi Energy it is about three. At Siemens Energy's grid business it is about four, and at Eaton, which makes the gear inside the fence, about one. All three have risen while the new factories were being announced.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>A popular hairdresser is booked weeks ahead. She adds a second chair, but only once the bookings justify it, and the diary still runs weeks ahead.</p>
<p>Counting her chairs tells you she is growing. Counting the weeks in her diary tells you whether you will get a cut sooner. For grid equipment, the diary runs in years.</p>
</details>

The makers even say how long it lasts. Siemens Energy estimates that in Europe and North America, demand for large transformers ran about 40% ahead of every maker's capacity in 2025. Its own chart still has demand ahead in 2030, by about 10%.

So the new factories, when they open from 2027, will meet a queue that has kept growing. The wait may get a little shorter in years, but it will not end this decade. For anyone planning a data centre, the transformer has to be booked before the site is chosen.

Hitachi Energy, by its own estimate the maker of one in six of the world's installed transformers, has $66.9bn of orders waiting, about 3.1 years of work. Siemens Energy's grid business has €51bn, about four years. Eaton's Electrical Americas, which sells the switchgear and power rooms inside the fence, passed a full year of sales at the end of 2025.

The levels are not comparable, and the direction is the point. The two grid makers carry HVDC links, high voltage direct current lines that take years to build, so their ratios sit higher by design. What matters is that all three rose together, in the same two years the factory programmes were announced.

Until those plants run, extra orders can only lengthen the queue. When they do, they add output to a pile that has grown in the meantime.

## The makers will not overbuild

The makers are not racing to empty the queue, and they say so. At its investor day on 10 June 2026, Hitachi showed its energy backlog growing towards about $100bn by fiscal 2030 and expected it to "remain stable at 2.5-3x" revenue. Hitachi Energy is "not building over-capacity", in Hitachi's own summary of the session.

The money is large and paced to orders. Hitachi Energy plans over $6bn of factory spending from 2024 to 2027, double the previous four years. Siemens Energy added about 20% capacity for €600m and is putting over €2bn more into transformers and switchgear by fiscal 2028. Both are building to the order book they can see, not to the one they hope for.

The industry's memory explains why. A transformer plant runs for decades, and the makers lived through long years when it stood half empty. Capacity sized to today's orders is safe; capacity sized to clear the queue would be idle the day demand cools.

## The wait already costs the makers

A queue sounds like pure profit for the seller, but orders priced months ago are delivered at today's costs. In the second quarter of 2026, Eaton's Electrical Americas margin fell two points year on year, to 27.5%, as higher metal costs met orders priced months earlier. The makers carry the risk of a long diary as well as its comfort, which is one more reason they will not build far ahead of it.

## What could shorten the wait

The case against is real. A narrowing gap can lower years of work while the pile still grows: if orders run 10% above output and output grows 6% a year, Siemens Energy's ratio drifts from 3.96 to about 3.7 within two years. Eaton's ratio rose by only 0.01 in its latest quarter. And price rises, early bookings and a stronger euro can hold a ratio up with no longer real wait, since no maker splits its backlog into price and volume.

<a id="schneider-sells-the-finished-system"></a>

Some buyers also route around the queue. Schneider Electric sells more power rooms built and tested in its factories, which cut the work electricians must do on site; my [claim on that](/claims/#schneider-sells-the-finished-system) is close to its line.

None of this ends the wait. A queue of three and a half years in 2028 binds a data centre developer as surely as four years today, and the makers' own plans are built to keep it. The second chair has arrived, and the diary still runs years ahead.

## The gear outlives the chip it was ordered for

<a id="the-equipment-queue"></a>

Here is the part that is easy to miss. Nvidia has launched a new flagship AI chip about every year: Blackwell in 2024, Blackwell Ultra in 2025 and Rubin in 2026. The order books for the gear that powers them run three to four years. Measured in chip launches, a transformer ordered today joins a pile of work that would take three or four generations to clear.

That turns the usual picture of an AI build upside down. The chip is the part a buyer can change late; the electrical gear is the part that must be settled first. A data centre's real start date is set when its transformer is booked, years before the chips it will run are on sale.

Whether the queue holds comes down to two things: whether the makers' years of work keep rising, and whether their new plants open on time. Put them together and every quarterly result lands in one box.

Today the marker sits at the top, between two boxes. Years of work are still rising, and the first plants are not due until 2027. My test draws the line: at 30 June 2028, Siemens Energy's grid business should still hold at least 3.6 years of work, and Eaton's Electrical Americas at least one.

The box it moves into decides who waits. In "kept on purpose", the developers who booked early switch on first and the late planners queue behind them. In "clearing", the advantage passes back to the buyers. Either way the same gear sits behind the grid stories in [America rations the plug](/journal/america-rations-the-plug/) and [the plug, not the chip](/journal/the-queue-not-the-chip/): a utility can clear a site's place in the grid queue, and the site still waits for its transformer.

Whether any of this is already in the makers' share prices is not a question this journal answers; that work sits in the research notes below.

## What would prove me wrong

The test fails if, at 30 June 2028, Siemens Energy's grid backlog is below 3.6 years of its sales or Eaton's Electrical Americas backlog is below one year. That is a genuine risk. The drift worked out above already takes Siemens Energy close to the line, and Eaton's new plants should be running well before the date.

A pass would not prove a longer wait on its own, since price, early bookings, currency and HVDC contracts can hold a ratio up. Hitachi Energy is left out because its own claim already tests its ratio, at 2.5, to 2031. Schneider publishes no backlog, so it cannot be tested this way.

Breaks if: "Systems falls below 34 per cent of Schneider's group revenue, its 2025 level, for the full year 2026 or 2027 while Schneider describes Data Centre and Networks sales as growing double digit; or Products outgrows Systems on an organic basis for two quarters running while data centres remain Schneider's main growth driver."

Where later evidence bears on it: not broken, but close. In the third quarter of 2026 Products grew 17% against 22% for Systems, and Schneider says Products growth was "majority volume-driven": more loose parts, not just higher prices. The nine-month Systems share is about 34% from rounded shares (33.9% to 34.9%), on the line. The share test checks the outcome, not the cause: buyers may want prefabrication for speed as much as for scarce electricians.

**Hitachi Energy is spending more than $9 billion on factories, and still plans a three-year queue** (22 October 2026)

Claim: "Hitachi Energy is adding factories only as fast as signed orders justify, and its order backlog will stay at or above about 2.5 times annual revenue, roughly three years of work, through fiscal 2030, which ends on 31 March 2031, so the queue for its transformers and HVDC links will not clear."

Breaks if: "Hitachi Energy's order backlog divided by its revenue over the previous four quarters, both in US dollars as Hitachi reports them (about 3.06 at 30 June 2026), falls below 2.5 at two consecutive quarter ends before 31 March 2031, the end of fiscal 2030; or its orders for any full fiscal year up to fiscal 2030 fall below its revenue for that year."

Where later evidence bears on it: the ratio rose to about 3.1 at 30 September 2026 (Hitachi, 28 October 2026). The claim holds. Its South Boston date came from trade press, not Hitachi.

**Eaton is shipping more electrical gear than ever, and its order book is growing faster still** (26 October 2026)

Claim: "Eaton is shipping more electrical gear than ever, yet its Electrical Americas backlog keeps growing faster than its sales, so the queue for the equipment inside a data centre's fence, measured in months of sales on order, will keep lengthening until its new plants arrive in 2027."

Breaks if: "Electrical Americas backlog divided by the segment's sales over the previous four quarters (1.05 at June 2026, up from 1.04 in March) falls for two consecutive quarters before mid 2027, when the new plants start; or falls below 0.93, its June 2025 level, before the end of 2027; or the segment's twelve-month book-to-bill falls below 1.0 for two quarters running; or Electrical Americas margin is still falling year on year after Eaton reports price and cost back to neutral."

Where later evidence bears on it: no new figures by 30 October 2026. The rise slowed to 0.01, and some of it may be price.

**Siemens Energy has measured the transformer shortage, and its own chart runs to 2030** (28 October 2026)

Claim: "By Siemens Energy's own estimate, demand for large and medium power transformers in Europe and North America exceeds the combined capacity of all market players in the region in every year through its fiscal 2030, which ends on 30 September 2030, so unfilled orders keep accumulating even as the gap narrows: Grid Technologies' orders stay above its revenue each fiscal year and its backlog in euros keeps growing."

Breaks if: "Before 30 September 2030: Grid Technologies' orders fall below its revenue for any full fiscal year; or its order backlog in euros, as Siemens Energy reports it (€51bn at 30 June 2026), falls for two consecutive quarters; or Siemens Energy publishes a revised estimate showing transformer capacity meeting demand in Europe and North America before fiscal 2030."

Where later evidence bears on it: no new figures by 30 October 2026. Its Charlotte slip rested on a press report; Siemens Energy's last stated date is early 2026, and its release of 3 February 2026 gives none.

The ratios are not like for like. The two grid makers carry HVDC links, high voltage direct current lines that take years to build. Eaton's gear mostly ships within a year. Direction matters, not comparison.
