This summer, a new factory in Houston that builds battery systems for Fluence was running on generators. Its own connection to the grid was late.

Fluence sells data centres a way to switch on before their grid connection is finished. The plant meant to supply its American orders had the same problem.

In August it averaged fewer than one unit a day, against a plan of eleven. Fluence cut its revenue forecast twice in six weeks.

Its factories overseas, the company said, kept working well. Demand at home and abroad, it said, stayed strong.

Fluence is not short of batteries. It is short of batteries that count as American.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a council that knocks a large sum off your bill if your new boiler was built in this country. Imported boilers are cheap and plentiful, and the shop has a warehouse full of them.</p>
<p>Everybody now wants the local kind. The one local workshop has a new welding robot that keeps jamming, so its staff weld each boiler by hand, three a day.</p>
<p>The shop is not short of boilers. It is short of boilers that earn the discount. That is Fluence in America in 2026.</p>

## Three words you need

**Integrator.** Fluence, which began as a joint venture of Siemens and the power company AES, does not make battery cells. It designs the system, has contract factories assemble it, and writes the software that runs it.

**Enclosure.** The large steel box that holds the battery modules, cooling and controls. The Houston plant welds these boxes and does final assembly. Fluence buys its output rather than owning it.

**Domestic content.** An American tax rule. A storage project already earns a tax credit worth a share of its cost, and a larger one if enough of the equipment is made in the United States.

A newer rule sits on top. New projects must also limit material from companies tied to China, Russia, Iran or North Korea, and that limit tightens every year.

## Why the label is part of the product

Fluence has built an American supply chain to meet those rules. It assembles battery modules in Utah, using cells made in Tennessee, and buys parts from several other states.

Tariffs push the same way. The American tariff on Chinese grid batteries more than tripled at the start of this year.

My reading is that an American buyer who wants the larger credit cannot be served from Vietnam or China, however well those lines run. Fluence's own filings warn that rivals may offer more American-made products, sooner, if its production slips.

In America the scarce thing is not the battery. It is the battery that qualifies, and Fluence can make that only on its American lines.

## What went wrong in Houston

On its August results call, Fluence said the plant would have capacity for 15 gigawatt hours a year. A gigawatt hour is a million kilowatt hours of stored energy, so this was a big step up for its American output.

Then the delays stacked up. Construction ran late, the automated equipment misbehaved, and the utility connection came late too. With the plant on generators, work that should have run side by side had to wait its turn.

In September Fluence cut its forecast again. Its release named delays at the Houston plant as the main reason, and said the international supply chain "has continued to work well".

Trade press reports of that day's call add the detail. Early September output was about three units a day, and most of the latest cut came from American production.

The custom automated welding ran well below target, so the contractor switched to welding by hand. By then, the reports say, power was no longer the limit. Welding was.

Note what failed. It was welding steel boxes and putting them together, not a shortage of battery cells.

## What data centres are actually buying

Fluence is a good test of my argument that grid batteries are becoming connection equipment. The evidence partly supports it.

This year Fluence signed its first order from a data centre developer, for a battery on the site's own side of the utility meter. In July a hyperscaler, one of the giant cloud companies, awarded it about $550 million more across several sites, though not yet as a signed order.

The chief executive said developers want "speed to power", and hyperscalers focus on the quality of that power. He said the software's ability to smooth out sudden swings in load had helped win the work.

That matches two jobs I have described for these batteries: letting a site open early, and absorbing the fast swings in AI power demand. Neither buyer, as he described them, is paying to trade electricity.

The size is where I have to correct myself. By my arithmetic, data centres are under a tenth of the work Fluence hopes to win, though the two figures may not be counted the same way. Utilities and independent power producers still make up most of its signed orders.

At Fluence, the data centre buyer is real and growing fast, but it is not yet the main customer.

## When the product is a date, lateness costs money

Fluence's contracts let customers charge liquidated damages, fixed penalties agreed in advance, when a delivery milestone is missed. Its latest quarterly report names them as a main reason its gross profit fell.

Its gross margin, the share of each sale left after the cost of making it, fell sharply on a year earlier. The report also blames cost overruns on newer products and dearer batteries.

I read that as a buyer paying for a delivery date, and a supplier handing back part of the price when the date slips.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Houston turns out to be a short teething fault. Suppose Fluence's published American production figures show Houston at about eleven units a day, or a 15 gigawatt hour a year pace, by the end of March 2027. If its plan for the year to September 2027 is then not cut again for American production, the factory was not the limit.

I am also wrong if demand binds first. If American orders fall while American lines have spare capacity, the label was never the scarce thing.

Fluence has said it will publish its American production levels, actual and forecast, so the test will be easy to read.
