This summer, a new factory in Houston that builds battery systems for Fluence was running on generators. Its own grid connection was late.

In August it averaged fewer than one finished battery enclosure a day, against a plan of eleven. Fluence cut its revenue forecast twice in six weeks.

Its factories overseas, the company said, kept working well. Demand, it said, stayed strong.

Fluence is not short of batteries. It is short of batteries that count as American.

<p>Everybody now wants the local kind, and the one local workshop's welding robot keeps jamming, so its staff weld by hand, three a day. The shop is short only of boilers that earn the discount.</p>

## Why the label is part of the product

Fluence is an integrator: it does not make battery cells, but designs the system, has contract factories assemble it and writes the software that runs it.

In America, a storage project earns a larger tax credit if enough of its equipment is made in the United States, a rule called domestic content. Tariffs push the same way. So a buyer who wants the larger credit cannot be served from Vietnam or China, however well those lines run.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Fluence's American systems depend on the Houston plant, which welds the enclosures, the large steel boxes that hold the battery modules. It was meant to reach 15 gigawatt hours a year, a gigawatt hour being a million kilowatt hours of storage. Construction ran late, and the automated welding ran so far below target that the contractor switched to welding by hand.

What failed was welding steel boxes, not a shortage of cells.

## What Fluence's own filings show

In September Fluence cut its forecast again. Its release named delays at the Houston plant as the main reason, and said the international supply chain "has continued to work well".

Its contracts let customers charge liquidated damages, fixed penalties agreed in advance, when a delivery date is missed. Its latest quarterly report names them as a main reason gross profit fell.

That is a buyer paying for a date, and a supplier handing back part of the price when the date slips. Data centres, which buy batteries [to open early](/journal/what-the-battery-is-really-for/), are among those buyers.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Houston is a short teething fault. If Fluence's published American production figures show it at about eleven units a day, or a 15 gigawatt hour pace, by the end of March 2027, and its plan for the year to September 2027 is not cut again, the factory was not the limit.

I am also wrong if American orders fall while American lines have spare capacity.
