This summer, a new factory in Houston that builds battery systems for Fluence was running on generators. Its own connection to the grid was late.

Fluence sells data centres a way to switch on before their grid connection is finished. The plant meant to supply its American orders had the same problem.

In August it averaged fewer than one unit a day, against a plan of eleven. Fluence cut its revenue forecast twice in six weeks.

Its factories overseas, the company said, kept working well. Demand at home and abroad, it said, stayed strong.

Fluence is not short of batteries. It is short of batteries that count as American.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a council that knocks a large sum off your bill if your new boiler was built in this country. Imported boilers are cheap and plentiful, and the shop has a warehouse full of them.</p>
<p>Everybody now wants the local kind. The one local workshop has a new welding robot that keeps jamming, so its staff weld each boiler by hand, three a day.</p>
<p>The shop is not short of boilers. It is short of boilers that earn the discount. That is Fluence in America in 2026.</p>

## Three words you need

**Integrator.** Fluence, which began as a joint venture of Siemens and the power company AES, does not make battery cells. It designs the system, has contract factories assemble it, and writes the software that runs it.

**Enclosure.** The large steel box that holds the battery modules, cooling and controls. The Houston plant welds these boxes and does final assembly. Fluence buys its output rather than owning it.

**Domestic content.** An American tax rule. A storage project already earns a tax credit worth a share of its cost. It earns a larger one if enough of the equipment is made in the United States.

A newer rule sits on top. Projects starting construction from 2026 must also limit material from what the law calls prohibited foreign entities, in short, companies tied to China, Russia, Iran or North Korea. That limit tightens every year.

## Why the label is part of the product

Fluence has built an American supply chain to meet those rules. Its annual report for the year to 30 September 2025 lists suppliers in Arizona, Texas, Tennessee, South Carolina and Utah.

Battery modules have been assembled in Utah since September 2024, using cells made in Tennessee. Fluence believes this lets it and its customers capture some of the tax incentives.

Tariffs push the same way. The American tariff on Chinese grid batteries rose from 7.5 to 25 per cent on 1 January 2026.

My reading is that an American buyer who wants the larger credit cannot be served from Vietnam or China, however well those lines run. Its own risk factors warn that rivals may offer American-made products in greater quantity and on a faster timeline if its own production slips.

In America the scarce thing is not the battery. It is the battery that qualifies, and Fluence can make that only on its American lines.

## What went wrong in Houston

On its August results call, Fluence said the plant would have capacity for 15 gigawatt hours a year, a large step up in its domestic content capacity.

Then the delays stacked up. Construction ran late, the automated equipment misbehaved, and the utility connection came late too. The chief executive said the plant ran on generators, so work that should have run in parallel had to wait.

The August cut was not only Houston. Fluence also blamed a new international contract factory, one of two new lines in China, whose first output needed rework for quality. By the August call it said that line was fixed and at full production.

On 16 September Fluence cut again. Revenue for the year to 30 September 2026 fell to about $2.4 billion, against an August midpoint of $3.0 billion and $3.4 billion before that.

The release named delays at the Houston contract manufacturing facility as the primary reason. It said demand had remained strong and the international supply chain "has continued to work well".

The unit figures come from trade press reports of the 16 September call. There, management reportedly said guidance had assumed eleven units a day. August averaged under one, and early September about three. About 80 per cent of the latest cut came from American production. The custom automated welding ran well below target, so the contractor switched to welding by hand.

By then, the reports say, generators were in place and power was no longer holding back output. The late grid connection had slowed the start. Welding was now the limit.

Note what failed. It was welding steel boxes and assembling them, not a shortage of battery cells.

## What data centres are actually buying

Fluence is a good test of my argument that grid batteries are becoming connection equipment. The evidence partly supports it.

In the quarter to 30 June 2026, Fluence signed its first order from a data centre developer. It was $300 million for a behind-the-meter project, meaning one on the site's own side of the utility meter. In July a hyperscaler, one of the giant cloud companies, awarded it about $550 million more across several sites. Those awards are not yet signed purchase orders.

The chief executive said developers want "speed to power" and took under three months from first contact to signed order. Hyperscalers, he said, focus on quality of power. He said the software's ability to smooth loads and handle periods of low voltage had contributed to the wins.

That matches two jobs I have described for these batteries: letting a site open early, and absorbing the fast swings in AI power demand. Neither buyer, as he described them, is paying to trade electricity.

The size is where I have to correct myself. Fluence put its data centre pipeline, the work it hopes to win, at 16 gigawatt hours. Its whole storage pipeline at 30 June was 163.7 gigawatt hours.

By my arithmetic that is under a tenth, though the two figures may not be counted the same way. Orders tell the same story. Of $2.7 billion signed in the nine months to 30 June, the chief executive said utilities and independent power producers made up about 90 per cent. At Fluence, the connection buyer is real and growing fast, but it is not yet the main customer.

## When the product is a date, lateness costs money

Fluence's contracts let customers charge liquidated damages, fixed penalties agreed in advance, when milestones are missed. Its quarterly report for the period to 30 June 2026 names them as a drag on revenue and a main reason gross profit fell.

Gross margin, the share of each sale left after the cost of making it, was 5.1 per cent that quarter. A year earlier it was 14.8 per cent. The report also cites cost overruns on newer products and higher battery prices.

I read that as a buyer paying for a delivery date, and a supplier handing back part of the price when the date slips.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Houston turns out to be a short teething fault. In August, Fluence expected full production there in the quarter to 31 December 2026. Suppose its published American production figures show Houston at about eleven units a day, or a 15 gigawatt hour a year pace, by the end of March 2027. If the plan for the year to September 2027 is then not cut again for American production, the factory was not the limit.

I am also wrong if demand turns out to bind first. If American orders fall while American lines have spare capacity, then the label was never the scarce thing.

Fluence has said it plans to publish its American production levels, actual and forecast. It plans to give a detailed plan for the year to September 2027 alongside its full-year results.

So I watch four things. Fluence's reported American production against plan. Any further cut to that plan for American reasons. Liquidated damages and gross margin in each quarterly filing. And whether the $550 million hyperscaler award becomes a signed order.
