This year three of the biggest buyers in AI have paid to secure the same thing from Corning. In January, Meta agreed to pay up to $6 billion for its fibre optic cable through 2030.

In May, Nvidia paid Corning to build more of it in America. In June, Amazon signed a supply deal of its own.

Corning makes no chips, lasers or switches. It made the glass for Edison's light bulb, and it makes the strand of glass between the chips.

Corning owns the part of the AI network that does not go out of date.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Think of the water pipes in your house. You replace the taps and the boiler several times, but the pipes inside the walls stay where they were laid.</p>
<p>In an AI data centre the boilers are the chips and the plugs, and every new one pushes twice as much through. The pipes are glass fibre. Corning lays the pipes, and every time the building grows, it needs more.</p>
</details>

## Why the glass is hard to replace

Training an AI model splits the work across thousands of chips that swap partial results constantly. At today's speeds a copper signal fades into noise within about two metres, roughly the height of a rack. Beyond that, data travels as light down optical fibre, a strand of glass thinner than a hair.

According to Corning, an AI rack of 72 chips needs about sixteen times more fibre than a traditional cloud switch rack. And the largest clusters now spread across campuses, so every new building must be stitched to all the others.

When links move from 800 gigabits a second to 1.6 terabits, the transceivers, the plugs at each end, are replaced. The fibre in the ceiling trays usually stays.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Purified silica is drawn into a strand at more than a kilometre a minute, its width held to millionths of a metre. A small error leaks light, and decades of that process knowledge are why a new entrant cannot simply buy its way in.

## What Corning's own results show

If the mechanism is real, Corning's fibre business should be outgrowing the rest of the company and earning more on each sale.

In the second quarter of 2026, its data centre fibre business grew by about two thirds on a year earlier. Profit in the wider fibre division grew more than twice as fast as its sales, and Corning says demand is running above what it can make.

Profit outrunning sales is what full factories and firm demand look like in money.

## What would prove me wrong

Corning is expanding hard, and so are producers in China. It has seen this before: the telecom fibre boom of the late 1990s ended in a huge loss in 2001.

So my test is simple. If the fibre division's profit margin falls while its sales keep rising, the scarcity is ending and I am wrong.
