Everyone says AI needs power. That is only half right, and the missing half changes who wins.

America is not running out of electricity. It is running out of the ability to deliver a large new block of it to one exact site, quickly.

A company can have the money, the chips and the land, and still be told to come back in five years. That wait is a shortage of wires, permits and transformers, not of fuel.

So look at what builders pay for when they go around the grid. Not cheaper electrons. Dearer ones, on purpose.

The product is not electricity. It is time.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>You move into a new flat and the broadband company says the engineer cannot come for three months. You work from home, so three months offline is not an option.</p>
<p>So you buy a mobile hotspot. Every gigabyte costs more than the cable would, and you know that when you buy it. You are not paying for better internet. You are paying to be online this week.</p>
<p>The day the engineer finally turns up, the hotspot goes into a drawer. Its whole reason to exist was the gap between when you needed the connection and when the cable could arrive. That gap is the market.</p>
</details>

## Three doors, and what each one costs

An AI builder with a site that needs hundreds of megawatts has roughly three ways to get them.

**The grid.** The cheapest power and the slowest to arrive. Joining means entering the interconnection queue, the waiting list to connect anything large. Reported waits run past four years, and up to seven in Northern Virginia.

**Your own gas turbine.** Proven and relatively cheap at scale. But as I have written before, this escape route has its own queue, because the turbine makers are largely sold out.

**Modular power built in a factory.** Boxes that arrive on a lorry, sit beside the building and make electricity there. Dearer per unit of energy, but the fastest installations are measured in months rather than years.

The third door only makes sense once you see the builder's real cost: an idle building full of chips that cannot switch on.

## What a fuel cell is, and why buyers pick it

The leading example of the third door is the solid oxide fuel cell. Think of a battery that never runs down, as long as you keep feeding it fuel. Natural gas goes in, electricity comes out, with no flame and no spinning machinery.

Inside, a thin ceramic plate is held at several hundred degrees. Oxygen from the air passes through it and reacts with the fuel on the other side, releasing electrons directly as current.

It is not solar, because it runs day and night. It is not a battery, because it makes power rather than storing it. And despite the reputation, it is not really a hydrogen product today. It can run on hydrogen, but nearly all of it runs on natural gas.

**Efficiency.** Skipping combustion turns around 54 to 60 per cent of the energy in the gas into electricity, against roughly 35 to 40 per cent for a typical simple gas turbine.

**Emissions, which is really a number about permits.** When Oracle switched its Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico from turbines to fuel cells, it cited about 92 per cent lower NOx, the nitrogen oxides that form smog. Less pollution means an easier air permit, and an easier permit means a faster start.

**Modularity.** Need more power, add more boxes, so a site can switch on in stages rather than waiting for one enormous machine.

None of this makes the electricity cheap. Buyers accept that, because they are buying the date.

## Where the time premium shows up in the numbers

Start with who is buying. Oracle has committed to up to 2.8 gigawatts of fuel cells, roughly the output of three large nuclear reactors, and at Project Jupiter they replaced the turbines as the primary power source. American Electric Power, one of the largest utilities in the United States, signed for up to 1 gigawatt. **The grid company itself is buying on-site generation, because even it cannot build wires fast enough.**

If those buyers really are paying for speed, the leading supplier's own figures should show it. Bloom Energy's do, in three places.

**Volume.** Revenue for the second quarter of 2026 was $1.07 billion, up 166 per cent on a year earlier. Product revenue, the boxes themselves, grew 215 per cent. That is equipment being delivered, not just announced.

**Margin.** Gross margin, the share of each sale left after the cost of building it, rose to 33.4 per cent from 26.7 per cent. A supplier that widens its margin while volume more than doubles is not discounting to win work. That is the time premium, expressed in money.

**The tail.** At the start of 2026 the company described about $20 billion of backlog, meaning orders signed but not yet delivered. Roughly $6 billion was product. The other $14 billion was contracts to maintain the boxes for up to twenty years.

That split matters. The premium for arriving early is paid once, at sale. The maintenance income keeps coming after the grid catches up, so it is the part least exposed to the gap closing.

## Why the advantage is real, and partly borrowed

The advantage is real, but it is not secret physics. It is years of manufacturing and deployment nobody else has done at this scale.

That is harder than it sounds. Bosch, one of Europe's most capable engineering firms, stopped developing solid oxide fuel cells in 2025. Ceres Power in the UK licenses its design to manufacturers rather than building systems itself.

The other half of the advantage is borrowed. Fuel cells win because the grid is slow and the turbines are sold out. Both of those can improve, and neither is under the fuel cell makers' control.

The boxes also still need gas, which means a pipeline connection with its own lead time and carbon rules that could change around the site. Time is bought, not conjured.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if fuel cells keep winning sites where a grid connection is available on schedule. That would mean they compete on the cost of energy, not on arrival date, which is a different market entirely.

I am also wrong in the other direction if fuel cell deployment times stretch towards the turbine and grid timelines. If the factory line becomes its own queue, the reason to pay extra disappears.

The numbers give a sharper test. If the premium is for time, gross margin should come under pressure as grid waits shorten. Margin holding while queues ease would mean buyers value something other than speed.

So I watch four things. The time from order to energised power at fuel cell sites. Grid and turbine waits, because shorter waits shrink the gap this market lives in. Gross margin against those waits. And whether fuel cells stay the primary supply at sites like Jupiter, or quietly become a bridge that is switched off once the grid arrives.
