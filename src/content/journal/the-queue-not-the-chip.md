SemiAnalysis, a research firm that follows the AI buildout, sorts the world into two kinds of place. America is "power-gated": its utilities face about a terawatt of requests, and, as the firm has put it since 2024, "the grid is sold out". China is "chip-gated", with power to spare and too few chips.

Singapore and Johor, the Malaysian state across the causeway, fit neither box neatly. They sit between them.

Singapore has the chips and no room for more power, so it rations who may plug in. Johor built its plugs so fast that campuses are connected years before tenants fill them.

In both, the plug, not the chip, decides who gets to build.

## How it works

A data centre needs more than electricity made somewhere. It needs a connection: a substation, the yard where high voltage power is stepped down, a transformer feeding it, and a signed place in the utility's plans.

That connection runs on two clocks. The first is paperwork: studies, approvals and agreeing who pays. The second is plant. A large power transformer is designed for its site, wound to order and tested before it ships, and its makers have years of orders waiting.

Malaysia's utility showed how far the whole wait can be squeezed. For data centres it cut the time to deliver a connection from up to four years to one.

Once connected, a hall runs on a third clock: filling it. The operator builds the shell, the power and the cooling, and the tenant brings the chips. For a tenant headquartered in China, those chips also need an American export licence, wherever the hall sits.

So supply cannot simply catch up. Singapore has no land for much more power, so it chooses who connects. Johor sped up its connections, and now waits on the third clock.

## The number that matters

In June 2026 the data centres in the system of Tenaga Nasional, Malaysia's grid utility, had booked 5.65 gigawatts of maximum demand. That is the most power a site may draw at once. They actually drew 1.26 gigawatts, less than a quarter.

The figure covers all of Malaysia, but Johor holds two thirds of its secured data centre capacity.

Part of the gap is normal, because a campus books for its finished size and fills in phases. Part is overbooking, which the government says it is studying. Part is chips.

Singapore shows the other side. In August it provisionally allocated 200 megawatts of new capacity to four operators, chosen from more than 20 proposals.

A booked megawatt is a place in line. It is not a computer at work.

## The mechanism in depth

Every new campus in Johor or Singapore passes three clocks in order. Only the first answers to a government.

| Clock | What it is | Who sets the pace | Can policy shorten it? | Evidence, dated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Permission and position | Studies, approvals, the supply agreement, who pays | The utility and the government | Yes, by decision | Tenaga's Green Lane Pathway cut the implementation period from 36 to 48 months to 12 (9 August 2023) |
| Plant | Transformers, switchgear, subsea cable, converter stations | The makers' factories | Only with new factories, which take years | Hitachi Energy's unfilled orders equal about three years of sales (June 2026); Siemens Energy sees transformer demand outrunning industry capacity every year to fiscal 2030 (20 November 2025) |
| Fill | Tenants' chips bought, shipped, racked and tested | Tenants, chip supply, US export licences | Not by Malaysia or Singapore | Malaysia's data centres drew 1,256 MW against 5.65 GW booked (June 2026) |

The power-gated and chip-gated split is SemiAnalysis's, which has itself called Inner Mongolia China's Johor. My addition is narrower: placing Johor and Singapore on their own regulators' figures.

Tenaga publishes the fill every quarter. Most briefings measure booked demand "in system"; one quotes capacity "in operation" instead, so that row is not strictly comparable.

| As at | Load drawn | Booked maximum demand | Share drawn | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| June 2025 | 603 MW | 3.5 GW in system, 24 projects | about 17% | Tenaga briefing, 2QFY2025 |
| December 2025 | 850 MW | about 4.5 GW in operation | about 19% | Tenaga briefing, 4QFY2025 |
| March 2026 | 1,054 MW | about 4.5 GW in system | about 23% | Tenaga briefing, 1QFY2026 |
| June 2026 | 1,256 MW | 5.65 GW in system, 42 projects | about 22% | Tenaga briefing, 28 August 2026 |
| August 2026 | about 1.37 GW | 6 GW at 45 operating sites; 8.771 GW signed by 62 | about 23% | Dewan Rakyat Hansard, 13 October 2026 |

Load drawn roughly doubled in the year to June 2026, while booked demand grew by about 60%, so the gap has narrowed only slightly. Behind it sit 2.21 GW under construction and 0.49 GW signed, 8.35 GW across 61 projects. Johor holds 29 of them and 5.6 of 8.3 GW of secured capacity. Every draw figure is Malaysia-wide; Tenaga publishes no Johor-only draw.

Nothing primary splits the gap between ramp, overbooking and chips. On 13 October 2026 the deputy minister told Parliament that data centres book more than they use, and that the Energy Commission is studying their real needs.

## Malaysia's queue: what Tenaga shortened

Tenaga launched the Green Lane Pathway on 8 August 2023 for data centres taking supply at 132 or 275 kilovolts. Its release promised connection "three times faster than the normal delivery time", cutting the implementation period from 36 to 48 months to 12.

A programme to compress connection time means connection was what stopped projects. That July argument still holds for a campus waiting to connect.

The release also undercuts my September argument. It cut the whole implementation period, plant included, not just the paperwork. So Malaysia has not shown the equipment clock binding on its own connections.

Where that clock does bind is in the makers' order books. [Hitachi Energy](/journal/the-equipment-queue/#hitachi-energy-plans-the-queue) expects its unfilled orders to stay at two and a half to three years of sales through fiscal 2030. [Siemens Energy's](/journal/the-equipment-queue/#siemens-energy-measures-the-shortage) chart covers Europe and North America, but the same factories and steel serve Johor. Whether Tenaga can keep its 12 months as the queue lengthens is the open question.

**Malaysia has shown the queue can be shortened. It has not yet had to show the factories can keep up.**

<a id="johor-plugs-in-before-it-fills"></a>
## Johor: plugged in faster than it fills

DayOne, one of Johor's largest operators, says in its US listing filing of 5 October 2026 that it provides "space, power and cooling" for customers' IT equipment. Its long take-or-pay contracts pay for capacity, while power is charged on customers' actual consumption as a pass-through. So a Johor hall can be energised, let and earning its capacity fees while drawing little.

Its largest customer, a global technology company with a leading short-form video platform, provided 69.4% of revenue in 2025 and 69.2% in the first half of 2026. SemiAnalysis names that customer, by committed power, as ByteDance. DayOne warns that restrictions on it could reduce demand for future deployments.

That is where the American rule bites. The US Bureau of Industry and Security confirmed on 31 May 2026 that exporting advanced computing items needs a licence for any entity headquartered in Country Group D:5, which includes China, or in Macau. The same holds where the ultimate parent is headquartered there, wherever the subsidiary sits. Operators need not stop ongoing use "until further notice".

Malaysia's own permit of 14 July 2025 covers exports, transhipments and transits of US-origin AI chips, not chips running in Johor. The licence follows the tenant's headquarters, not the hall's address.

This narrows my July claim rather than reversing it. For a campus waiting to connect, the wire is scarce. For one already plugged in, tenants' chips set much of the pace, partly decided in Washington.

<a id="malaysia-keeps-the-wires-shared"></a>
## Malaysia's bill: who pays for the plug

If campuses book far more than they draw, someone pays for idle wires. Tenaga's own terms split the plug in two.

| Charge | Data centres against ordinary high voltage users | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Energy, per unit used | 55.18 and 51.09 sen per kWh, peak and off-peak, about a quarter higher | Energy Commission schedule, from 1 July 2025 |
| Capacity and network, per kW of maximum demand | RM21.76 and RM23.06 per kW, the same | Energy Commission schedule, from 1 July 2025 |
| Connected load charge | RM8.50 per kW of shortfall below 85% of phased declared demand, years one to four, for all new medium and high voltage customers | Tenaga charges terms |
| The campus's own connection | Project cost less 15 years of the customer's own tariff margin at 7.3%, or a refundable 25% deposit forfeited below 75% of final demand within six years | Tenaga Connection Charge Book 2025 |

The higher energy rate is the ultra high voltage class, for data centres connected above 50 kilovolts (Dewan Negara, 8 December 2025). Each campus's own connection is billed to its developer, net of what its bills should repay. The shared network behind it stays in the regulated tariff, including up to RM16.27 billion of contingent spending for 2025 to 2027, "activated upon trigger events". Meeting new demand from data centres with signed supply agreements is one listed example (Tenaga investor deck, October 2026).

The next tariff period, set in 2027 for 2028 to 2032, could change the split. On 15 October 2026 the energy minister told the Dewan Rakyat the regulator will study a user-pays basis, naming data centres.

<a id="johor-waits-on-the-treatment-plant"></a>
## Johor's water: a margin set by treatment plants

| Year | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Johor reserve margin | 14.3% | 11.5% | 16.8% | 15.7% | 14.3% |

Water runs on a plant clock too: the reserve margin, spare treatment capacity above demand in SPAN's Water and Sewerage Fact Book 2025, jumps when a plant opens and is eaten back within a couple of years. SPAN's target is a band of 10 to 15%, the ministry told Parliament in November 2019, so Johor in 2025 sat inside it and water is not its gate today. I held a claim that the margin would stay under 15%, because SPAN's own series did not support it.

<a id="two-governments-one-confession"></a>
## Singapore rations what Malaysia builds

Singapore's second Data Centre Call for Application, opened on 1 December 2025, offered at least 200 MW. Bidders needed a PUE of 1.25 or better at full IT load, at least 50% green power and the top Green Mark rating. PUE, power usage effectiveness, is power entering the building divided by power reaching the computers.

On 21 August 2026 EDB and IMDA provisionally allocated 50 MW each to Digital Realty, Equinix, Keppel Data Centres and STT GDC, from more than 20 proposals, all on Jurong Island. Singapore already has more than 1.6 GW of data centre capacity (MDDI, 8 September 2026). The ministry sets no fixed ceiling, only growth "within the envelope of our resource constraints" (3 March 2026).

Malaysia's side is less clean than I wrote in September. Its base grid allowance for 2025 to 2027 is RM26.55 billion, of which RM3.49 billion, 13%, is labelled demand growth, data centres included. The rest is energy transition and security of supply (Tenaga investor deck, October 2026). Data centre connection spending sits mostly in the contingent allowance, which is released only on triggers.

One line was also too strong. I wrote that Singapore cannot make more. Its regulator plans five gas power stations, lifting capacity by about 26% from about 12 GW by 2031 (EMA, 26 October 2026). It still cannot find land or much green power, and data centres still get their share by ration.

**Nobody rations something that is plentiful.**

<a id="singapore-cannot-wait-for-the-cable"></a>
## Singapore's green power: the cable cannot come in time

Singapore aims to import about 6 GW of low-carbon power by 2035 (EMA, 30 May 2025). By 7 August 2026 it had 13 import projects, every one conditional, with developers' dates running from 2028 at the earliest to 2035. Each needs subsea cable and converter stations built to order by the makers above. The data centre call did not wait for them: its green pathways, listed "such as" clean fuels and building solar, did not mention imports. Neither agency gives a reason, so that reading is mine.

<a id="singapore-licenses-the-old-halls"></a>
## Singapore's old halls: a licence by the megawatt

The ration now reaches running halls. The Digital Infrastructure Bill, Bill No. 20 of 2026, was debated at Second Reading on 6 and 7 October. The minister's closing speech refers to "the approved Act", expected in force in 2027.

| Provision | What it does | Clause |
|---|---|---|
| Licence | Needed for any hall of 3 MW or more of critical IT load, own-use halls included | 2, 23 |
| Stated capacity | Each licence states the critical IT load the hall may run | 26(2) |
| Efficiency duty | Minimum efficiency "as may be prescribed", measured by PUE | 35 |
| Enforcement | A fine, or a cut to licensed critical IT load | 39(2)(b), 40 |

About 70 data centres are in scope, Senior Minister of State Tan Kiat How said on 7 October. The Bill holds no PUE level and no date; both await IMDA regulations. Pacing will follow "investment and depreciation cycles". For investors the lever is plain: a Singapore hall's megawatts become a licensed quantity the regulator can cut.

## Claims on record

Each claim is quoted word for word from the claims page, with the date its piece went live.

**The queue, not the chip, is what's holding AI back** (27 July 2026)

Claim: "In Johor and Singapore, a finished grid connection, not chip supply, is the scarce thing holding the AI buildout back."

Breaks if: "Connection waits keep shrinking the way Malaysia's Green Lane cut them from 36 months to 12, or transformer and cable lead times normalise faster than utilities commit capital."

Where later evidence cuts against it: Malaysia's connected data centres, two thirds of whose secured capacity is in Johor, draw less than a quarter of what they booked. For campuses already connected, a connection is not the scarce thing. The claim holds for Singapore and for campuses still waiting to connect. I found no published cut below 12 months, and Tenaga's own 2023 release gave the starting point as 36 to 48 months.

**The one part of the grid that money cannot hurry** (22 September 2026)

Claim: "A grid connection queue is really two queues. Governments can shorten the paperwork one, but the equipment one sets the pace."

Breaks if: "Connection timelines keep compressing across the board, or equipment lead times normalise faster than utility spending is committed. Also if building power on site removes the constraint rather than moving it."

Where I was wrong: Tenaga's 2023 release says the Green Lane cut the whole implementation period from 36 to 48 months to 12, not only the paperwork. So in Malaysia's own connections, the equipment clock has not been shown to set the pace. The makers' order books still run years long, but the evidence I cited does not show them binding here.

**Two governments did the opposite thing and confessed the same problem** (22 September 2026)

Claim: "Malaysia building more grid and Singapore rationing who may connect are opposite answers to the same shortage of connection capacity."

Breaks if: "Malaysia's spending turns out to be ordinary renewal weighted towards generation rather than connection, or Singapore's efficiency ceiling moves on a fixed schedule rather than with scarcity, or a third constrained market does neither."

Where later evidence cuts against it: the allowance is grid, not generation, but only 13% of Tenaga's base grid allowance for 2025 to 2027 is labelled demand growth. Most is energy transition and security of supply, closer to renewal than to new connection, with data centre spending in a contingent allowance released on triggers. That weakens the Malaysian half. The piece also said Singapore cannot make more; it is building five gas power stations by 2031, though it still rations data centres' share.

<a id="the-plug-not-the-chip"></a>

**New: Johor and Singapore, the plug, not the chip** (30 October 2026)

Claim: "In Johor and Singapore the grid connection, not the chip, decides who gets to build: Malaysia's data centres, two thirds of whose secured capacity is in Johor, will draw less than 40% of the maximum demand of the data centre projects in Tenaga's system at June 2027, and Singapore will add data centre capacity only through calls for applications with efficiency and green energy conditions."

Breaks if: "Tenaga's figures for June 2027 show Malaysia's data centre load at 40% or more of the maximum demand of the data centre projects in its system, or, before 1 January 2028, Singapore allocates capacity to a new data centre, or new capacity to an existing one, other than through a call for applications carrying efficiency and green energy conditions. Capacity regained by redeveloping or replacing an existing hall within its current allocation, and capacity allocated before 30 October 2026, do not count."

## What would prove me wrong

The first is Tenaga's briefing for June 2027. If Malaysia's data centres draw 40% or more of the maximum demand in its system, halls are filling about as fast as they connect. On the trend, load doubling again to about 2.5 GW while most of the 2.21 GW under construction enters the system gives roughly a third. Under 40% would not by itself show chips are the cause, since ramp and overbooking fit it as well.

The second is Singapore. If it allocates new data centre capacity before 2028 outside a call with efficiency and green power conditions, it is no longer rationing the plug.
