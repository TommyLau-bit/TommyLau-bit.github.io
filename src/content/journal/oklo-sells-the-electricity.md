Almost every company building small nuclear reactors wants to sell you one. Oklo does not.

Oklo was founded by two nuclear engineers who met at MIT. It plans to build its reactors with its own money, place them beside a customer's data centre, and keep them.

What it sells is the electricity, every month, under contracts meant to run for decades. That makes Oklo a power company, not an equipment maker. It also means it earns nothing from power until its first reactor runs, and it has not finished one.

Oklo is not selling reactors. It is selling decades of electricity from reactors it has yet to build.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Most offices that want decent coffee buy an espresso machine. They pay once, and from then on the beans, the descaling and the breakdowns are their problem.</p>
<p>Some suppliers offer a different deal. They install the machine, keep owning it, fill it and fix it, and charge the office for every cup, for years. The supplier is paid only while coffee comes out.</p>
<p>That supplier has a harder first year and a better tenth. It pays for the machine up front and then waits for the cups. Oklo wants to be that supplier, except the machine is a nuclear reactor, and it has not poured a cup yet.</p>

## Three ideas you need

**Small modular reactor.** A nuclear reactor built from standard factory-made parts, rather than as a one-off giant like the plants of the past.

**Power purchase agreement.** A contract in which a buyer agrees to take electricity at a set price, often for decades. People shorten it to PPA.

**Behind the meter.** Generating on the customer's own side of the utility meter, right beside the building, so the site never waits in the grid's connection queue.

## Why a data centre wants a reactor next door

An AI data centre needs a large block of power at one exact site, around the clock. The grid can take years to connect it, gas turbines are sold out, and solar and wind stop with the weather.

What the largest buyers want is firm power with no carbon, delivered next to the building. Existing nuclear plants offer that, which is why cloud companies have been signing deals with their owners. Oklo's pitch is new plants small enough to sit on the data centre's own land.

Its rivals, such as NuScale and GE Vernova Hitachi, sell reactor designs and equipment to utilities. The utilities then carry the risk of building and running them.

Oklo inverts that. It raises the money, builds the reactor, owns it and sells the output under a power purchase agreement. There is no revenue on the day of the sale, because there is no sale.

There is a power bill every month, for decades, once the plant runs. The catch is that Oklo carries every risk a buyer otherwise would: construction cost, delay, fuel and licensing.

## What Oklo actually builds

Oklo's reactor is called Aurora. The first, at Idaho National Laboratory, is designed for 75 megawatts of electricity. It is a fast reactor, one that runs on unslowed neutrons, and it is cooled by liquid sodium rather than water.

That choice matters physically. Water must be held under enormous pressure to stay liquid at reactor temperatures, which is why conventional plants need massive steel vessels. Sodium stays liquid at ordinary pressure, so the plant can be simpler and smaller.

The design is old on purpose. It descends from an experimental reactor that ran in Idaho for three decades. In a famous test, operators stopped its coolant pumps with the automatic shutdown disabled, and the reactor settled down on its own.

So the physics has passed its exam. What has not been tested is whether anyone can build these reactors in numbers and on budget. Small reactors fell out of favour in the West because they cost too much, not because they failed.

The model works only if the tenth Aurora is much cheaper than the first.

## What Oklo's own reports show

If the model is what Oklo says, the early picture should show heavy spending, almost no income, and customers slowly putting money behind the paper. That is what its reports show.

Oklo is spending heavily on its first plants and losing money while it does. It is funded by investors, through new shares, rather than by customers. Its small revenue comes from engineering businesses it recently bought, not from electricity.

The customer commitments are mostly paper so far. Oklo's own filings describe most of them as non-binding. Its large framework agreement with Switch, a data centre developer, needs binding contracts to follow as projects reach milestones.

Meta is the exception. Its January 2026 agreement for a campus in Ohio includes a binding prepayment to secure fuel. That is the first customer money placed ahead of the reactor.

## The two things that can stop it

**Fuel.** Aurora runs on HALEU, high-assay low-enriched uranium, which is enriched much further than the fuel in ordinary reactors. The main commercial seller was Russia, and that route has closed. Oklo's first core uses fuel recovered from the old Idaho reactor. Beyond that, it relies on a letter of intent with Centrus Energy, an American enrichment company.

**Licence.** America's nuclear regulator, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, turned down Oklo's first licence application for lack of safety information. Oklo now runs two tracks. Its Idaho plant sits on federal land under the Department of Energy, which targets operation in 2028. Commercial sites like Ohio still need the full licence.

## What would prove me wrong

My claim is that Oklo is a power seller rather than a reactor maker, and that this model stands or falls on building its first plant.

I am wrong on the first half if Oklo starts selling Auroras outright to raise cash, because that concedes the model.

I am wrong on the second half if Aurora in Idaho slips well past 2028, or if its power is uncompetitive with gas. I am also wrong if the letters of intent stay letters. Every year Oklo waits, a buyer's need is met by existing nuclear, gas or fuel cells instead.

So I watch four things. The pace of construction in Idaho. The first non-binding agreement becoming a binding power purchase agreement. A firm HALEU supply contract. And how much new capital Oklo needs before it sells its first unit of electricity.
