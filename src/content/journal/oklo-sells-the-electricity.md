Almost every company building small nuclear reactors wants to sell you one. Oklo does not.

Oklo was founded in 2013 by two nuclear engineers who met at MIT. It plans to build its reactors with its own money, place them beside a customer's data centre, and keep them.

What it sells is the electricity, every month, under contracts meant to run for decades. That makes Oklo a power company, not an equipment maker. It also means it earns nothing from power until its first reactor runs, and it has not finished one.

Oklo is not selling reactors. It is selling decades of electricity from reactors it has yet to build.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Most offices that want decent coffee buy an espresso machine. They pay once, and from then on the beans, the descaling and the breakdowns are their problem.</p>
<p>Some suppliers offer a different deal. They install the machine, keep owning it, fill it and fix it, and charge the office for every cup, for years. The supplier is paid only while coffee comes out.</p>
<p>That supplier has a harder first year and a better tenth. It pays for the machine up front and then waits for the cups. Oklo wants to be that supplier, except the machine is a nuclear reactor, and it has not poured a cup yet.</p>

## Four words you need

**SMR.** A small modular reactor: a nuclear reactor of up to about 300 megawatts, built from standard factory-made parts rather than as a one-off giant.

**PPA.** A power purchase agreement, a contract in which a buyer agrees to take electricity at a set price, often for 10 to 40 years.

**Independent power producer.** A company that owns power plants and sells their output without owning the wires that carry it. That is what Oklo intends to be.

**Behind the meter.** Generating on the customer's own side of the utility meter, right beside the building, so the site never waits in the grid's connection queue. I covered the same route with Bloom Energy's fuel cells.

## Why a data centre wants a reactor next door

An AI data centre needs a large block of power at one exact site, around the clock. The grid can take five years or more to connect it, gas turbines are sold out, and solar and wind stop with the weather.

What the largest buyers want is firm power with no carbon, delivered next to the building. Existing nuclear plants offer that, which is why cloud companies have been signing deals with their owners. Oklo's pitch is new plants small enough to sit on the data centre's own land.

Its rivals, such as NuScale and GE Vernova Hitachi, sell reactor designs and equipment to utilities, which then carry the risk of building and running them. Oklo inverts that. It raises the money, builds the reactor, owns it and sells the output under a PPA.

There is no revenue on the day of the sale, because there is no sale. There is a power bill every month, for decades, once the plant runs. The catch is that Oklo carries every risk a buyer otherwise would: construction cost, delay, fuel and licensing.

## What Oklo actually builds

Oklo's reactor is called Aurora. The first, at Idaho National Laboratory, is designed for 75 megawatts of electricity. It is a fast reactor, one that runs on unslowed neutrons, and it is cooled by liquid sodium rather than water.

That choice matters physically. Water must be held under enormous pressure to stay liquid at reactor temperatures, which is why conventional plants need massive steel pressure vessels. Sodium stays liquid at those temperatures at ordinary pressure, so the plant can be simpler and smaller.

The design is old on purpose. It descends from EBR-II, the Experimental Breeder Reactor II, which ran in Idaho from 1964 to 1994. In tests in 1986, operators stopped the coolant pumps with the automatic shutdown disabled. The hot metal fuel expanded, the chain reaction slowed, and the reactor settled down on its own.

So the physics has passed its exam. What has not been tested is whether anyone can build these reactors in numbers and on budget. Small reactors fell out of favour in the West because they cost too much, not because they failed. The model works only if the tenth Aurora is much cheaper than the first.

## What Oklo's own numbers show

If the model is what Oklo says, the early numbers should show heavy spending on plants, almost no income, and customers slowly putting money behind the paper.

**Spending.** In the six months to 30 June 2026, Oklo reported a net loss of $81.6 million and capital spending of $126.9 million. It guides to $400 to $500 million of capital spending for 2026. That is what building before selling looks like.

**Funding.** Cash and marketable securities stood at $3.0 billion at 30 June, about $1.9 billion more than at the start of the year, raised mainly by issuing new shares. Until plants run, investors fund the build, not customers.

**Revenue.** Second quarter revenue was $1.2 million, from engineering businesses Oklo recently bought, not from electricity. Its Groves reactor in Texas reached criticality, the point where a chain reaction sustains itself, in August 2026. Groves makes medical and industrial isotopes, and Oklo expects its first isotope revenue in early 2027.

**Contracts.** Oklo's filings describe most customer commitments as non-binding. The 12 gigawatt master agreement with Switch, the data centre developer, signed in December 2024, is a framework, with binding contracts to follow as projects reach milestones. Meta is the exception. Its January 2026 agreement for a campus of up to 1.2 gigawatts in Pike County, Ohio, includes a binding prepayment to secure fuel.

## The two things that can stop it

**Fuel.** Aurora runs on HALEU, high-assay low-enriched uranium, enriched to between 5 and 20 per cent, against under 5 per cent in conventional reactors. The main commercial seller was Russia, and that route has closed. Oklo's first core is covered by five tonnes of HALEU recovered from old EBR-II fuel. Beyond that it relies on a letter of intent with Centrus Energy, signed in June 2026, for fuel for up to five Auroras from 2029.

**Licence.** In January 2022 the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, America's nuclear regulator, denied Oklo's first licence application for lack of safety information. Oklo now runs two tracks. Its Idaho plant sits on federal land under the Department of Energy, which approved its preliminary safety analysis in June 2026 and targets operation in 2028. Commercial sites like Ohio still need the full NRC licence.

## What would prove me wrong

My claim is that Oklo is a power seller rather than a reactor maker, and that this model stands or falls on building. I am wrong on the first half if Oklo starts selling Auroras outright to raise cash, because that concedes the model.

I am wrong on the second half if Aurora in Idaho slips well past 2028, or if its cost leaves the power uncompetitive with gas. I am also wrong if the letters of intent stay letters. Existing nuclear, gas and fuel cells are being contracted today, and every year Oklo waits, a buyer's need is met elsewhere.

So I watch four things. The pace of construction in Idaho. The first non-binding agreement becoming a binding PPA. A firm HALEU supply contract. And how much new capital Oklo needs before it sells its first unit of electricity.
