In 1979, one of the two reactors at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania partly melted down. The other ran on, after a long shutdown, until 2019, when it closed because it was losing money.

Constellation is now bringing that second reactor back under a new name, Crane, with Microsoft buying its power for twenty years.

The reactor can be ready in 2027. A fresh grid connection would not have been fully ready until around 2030 or later.

For Constellation, the scarce thing is not the reactor. It is the place on the grid the reactor plugs into.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a house left empty for a few years. The boiler and the wiring are still there, but the electricity account was closed and the meter taken away. A plumber can fix the boiler in weeks.</p>
<p>Getting the power company to reconnect the house can take far longer. The quick way in is to move the connection over from another house you own and meant to close.</p>
</details>

## How a restart works

Crane is an 835 megawatt reactor, enough for a large AI campus that runs around the clock. Constellation has estimated the restart would cost about $1.6 billion, for parts, inspections, staff and approval from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the federal safety regulator.

That is the familiar part. The unfamiliar part is the grid. When the plant closed, it gave up its place on the grid, so Constellation had to apply to PJM, the operator of the regional grid, like any new power plant.

PJM's study tied the connection to hundreds of miles of new transmission lines, the high-voltage wires that carry power across the region. Those lines would not be finished before the end of 2030, so full connection could wait until then or later.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Only three closed American reactors, Crane, Palisades and Duane Arnold, have restart plans, and new reactors, like [Oklo's](/journal/oklo-sells-the-electricity/), are a bet on the 2030s. Uprates, new turbines and parts that squeeze more power from a running reactor, help, but only as far as each design allows.

## Where the borrowed connection shows up

Constellation's answer was to borrow a connection it already owned. It asked federal regulators to let it move 760 megawatts of grid rights to Crane from two ageing oil and gas units at its Eddystone plant, near Philadelphia. It had planned to retire them, and they now run only under federal emergency orders.

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission agreed in June 2026, and Constellation still targets a 2027 restart.

Its next move points the same way. On 6 October it agreed with Google to add 890 megawatts by upgrading eleven reactors it already runs. Amanda Peterson Corio of Google put it plainly: the fastest new megawatt "is often the one that can be provided by existing infrastructure".

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Crane, with its borrowed connection in hand, has still not sent power to the grid at full output by the end of 2028. That would mean the reactor, not the wire, was the slow part.

I am also wrong if restarts or uprates that need new grid capacity get connected as quickly as those that reuse an existing place on the grid.
