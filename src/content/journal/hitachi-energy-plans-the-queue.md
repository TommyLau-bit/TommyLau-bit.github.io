At the end of June 2026, Hitachi Energy had $63.6 billion of orders signed but not yet delivered. That is roughly three years of its sales.

It is spending more than $9 billion on more than 40 new and expanded factories, the largest programme in its industry.

Yet its parent, Hitachi, expects the queue to stay at two and a half to three years of sales through its 2030 financial year.

Hitachi Energy is not building its way out of the transformer queue. It is building just fast enough to keep it.

## Factories that follow the orders

Hitachi Energy is the power grids business of Hitachi, the Japanese industrial group. It makes large power transformers, the machines that step voltage down at a substation, each built to order around a core of specialist steel.

It also makes HVDC links, high voltage direct current lines that carry large amounts of power over long distances, and switchgear, which makes and breaks high voltage connections safely.

Orders keep arriving faster than the factories can turn them into machines. Hitachi says orders from data centres more than doubled last year.

New capacity is slow. A transformer plant at South Boston, Virginia, broke ground in June and should operate by 2028. That is [the transformer clock](/journal/the-part-money-cannot-hurry/): a factory announced today makes its first transformer two to three years later.

Hitachi Energy is not trying to get ahead of demand either. Andreas Schierenbeck, who runs the business, said "we are not building over-capacity". A plant runs for decades, and a long queue protects it from the empty years the industry remembers. I read that as deliberate, and sensible.

## What the five-year plan says

Hitachi reports the backlog, orders firmly placed but not yet received, every quarter. Dividing it by a year of revenue turns it into years of work, and by June that stood at just over three.

At its June investor day, Hitachi said Hitachi Energy's revenue nearly doubles by the end of its plan. The backlog is expected to "remain stable" at two and a half to three times revenue.

In other words, it plans to nearly double its output and still have close to three years of work waiting.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Hitachi Energy's backlog falls below 2.5 times its revenue over the previous four quarters, at two quarter ends in a row, before 31 March 2031. Today it is just over three.

I am also wrong if its orders for any full fiscal year before then fall below its revenue. The queue would then be worked down, not kept.
