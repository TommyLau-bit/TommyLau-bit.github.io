Seagate shipped about two fifths more high-capacity storage in its last financial year than the year before. It did not get there by making many more hard drives.

It packed more data onto each spinning disk instead. Its own chief executive says Seagate is "not really increasing the box count".

Meanwhile AI keeps producing data that someone has to keep, and most of what Seagate can make is already spoken for into 2028.

Seagate sells terabytes, not drives, and the terabytes are taken.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Think of a removals firm with a fixed number of vans. As bookings grow, it does not buy more vans. It learns to pack each one tighter, a few more boxes every year, and customers book their move a year ahead.</p>
<p>More vans would clear the waiting list. But the firm remembers years when its vans sat idle in the yard, so it packs tighter instead, and the list stays full.</p>
</details>

## How a drive gets bigger

A hard drive is a sealed box of spinning metal disks, called platters, with tiny magnetic heads reading and writing just above them. Data centres buy nearline drives, the high-capacity kind that hold data kept rather than constantly used.

AI fills them. Training data, saved snapshots of a model part way through training, and answers kept for later all need somewhere to live. For data that is kept rather than read all day, the spinning drive is still the cheapest home per terabyte. Seagate says hard drives hold the vast majority of the data shipped into large data centres.

Seagate grows by raising areal density, how much data fits on each patch of disk. Its Mozaic drives use HAMR, heat-assisted magnetic recording: a tiny laser warms a spot on the disk for an instant so it can hold a smaller magnetic bit. Its newest Mozaic drives hold up to 44 terabytes.

Supply cannot simply catch up. Only Seagate, Western Digital and Toshiba make these drives, and Seagate's factory tools are custom made with long lead times. Each new generation must also pass qualification, the testing every cloud customer runs before trusting a part. I read the preference for denser drives over more drives as deliberate, and rational, after past gluts.

## Same drives, more terabytes

Seagate's January results put it plainly. In the last three months of 2025 it shipped 26 per cent more storage than a year earlier, "on similar number of units".

The commitments moved out faster than the calendar. In January, Seagate said its nearline output was allocated through 2026. By July, its chief executive said the vast majority was "now allocated into calendar 2028". Its finance chief added that the gap between supply and demand was "a little bit bigger" than a few quarters earlier.

That is what supply looks like when it grows only by the terabyte.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Seagate starts growing supply by making many more drives: a new drive factory, or drive numbers rising faster than terabytes per drive.

I am also wrong if, at any quarterly results before the end of 2027, Seagate says most of its nearline output for the next calendar year is not yet committed. Either would mean the terabyte is no longer what binds.
