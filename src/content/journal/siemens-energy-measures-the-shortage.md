Siemens Energy has published something I rarely see from an equipment maker: how far demand for large transformers runs ahead of the factories.

The chart appeared at its capital markets day last November, a briefing where a company sets out its plans to investors. It counts every maker of large and medium power transformers in Europe and North America, not only Siemens Energy itself.

Siemens Energy's fiscal year, its financial year, ends on 30 September. On that chart, demand ran about 40 per cent ahead of the whole industry's capacity in fiscal 2025. In fiscal 2030, after five more years of factory building, demand is still about 10 per cent ahead.

Most readers would call that a shortage coming to an end. On the chart's own terms, it is a shortage that leaves more orders waiting every single year.

By Siemens Energy's own count, the transformer shortage narrows to 2030, and the pile of orders waiting for it still grows.

## What Siemens Energy makes for the grid

Siemens Energy is the German energy equipment group that Siemens separated from itself and listed on the stock market. It makes gas and wind turbines, but this piece is about its grid business, which it calls Grid Technologies.

**Power transformers.** The machine at the centre of a substation, which steps voltage down from the level used for long distance transport to one a site can use. Siemens Energy says one in five of those installed worldwide came from its factories.

**Switchgear.** The heavy equipment that makes and breaks high voltage connections safely, so one fault does not take down the grid around it.

**HVDC links.** High voltage direct current links carry bulk power over long distances with less loss. Siemens Energy also builds whole substations as projects.

A data centre cannot connect to the grid without a transformer. In its results for the quarter to June, Siemens Energy said transformers, including data centre projects, made the largest contribution to Grid's order growth.

## The chart that counts the whole industry

The slide's subheading is plain: "Market remains tight". It measures transformers in gigavolt-amperes, GVA, a unit for how much power a transformer can carry.

By my reading of the bars, the industry nearly doubles its capacity over the years the chart covers. Demand still finishes ahead.

Siemens Energy is doing its share. It says recent spending on its factories added about a fifth to its own transformer capacity, and it plans to spend much more. At Nuremberg in Germany, where it has made transformers since 1912, it is adding about half again by 2028.

New floor space is slow to arrive. When Siemens Energy announced its first American large transformer plant, at Charlotte, North Carolina, it said production would begin in early 2026. Last year Tim Holt, the board member responsible for the grid business, told Reuters it would be early 2027.

## A narrower gap still adds to the pile

Here is the part the chart does not say out loud. If yearly demand stays above yearly output, the orders left unfilled each year are added to those already waiting.

A wide gap adds to the pile quickly. A narrow gap adds slowly. Neither takes anything away from it, so the pile only stops growing when the gap reaches zero.

That reading is mine. It assumes the chart compares yearly demand with yearly output, which the slide does not state. Capacity may also count only factories inside the region, so imports could fill part of the gap.

It does not mean the wait in years gets longer. Faster factories can shorten the wait while the pile still grows. My claim is only about the pile.

Siemens Energy's own order book behaves the way the chart implies. Grid's backlog, meaning signed orders still to be built and shipped, has risen every year, to €51 billion in June 2026. In the nine months to June 2026, Grid took in new orders nearly twice as fast as it delivered them.

The fit is not exact. The chart covers transformers in Europe and North America, while Grid's backlog is global and includes long HVDC and offshore projects. It is also counted in euros, so a weaker dollar shrinks American orders on paper. My tests use Grid's global figures as a stand-in for the chart, so one large HVDC order can move them either way.

## Grid against gas, inside one company

Siemens Energy's gas turbine and grid businesses sell to many of the same buyers, and report side by side.

Gas Services earns much of its money servicing turbines already running, which usually pays well. Grid sells almost nothing but new equipment, which usually pays less.

Yet Grid now earns the higher profit margin, the share of each sale left as profit before one-off costs. A few years ago its margin was thin. Gas is still working through older turbine orders signed at lower prices, which holds its margin down for now.

My reading is that buyers are paying more for scarce grid equipment than for the turbines themselves. Siemens Energy credits Grid's gains to the "improved margin profile of the processed order backlog", meaning orders signed once the shortage was plain.

Grid already earns more than the margin Siemens Energy once set as its goal for 2028. Siemens Gamesa, the wind business, shows the reverse, with weak orders and a small loss. A tough comparison with large offshore orders a year earlier explains part of that, so I treat it as a contrast, not proof.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Grid Technologies' orders for any full fiscal year before 30 September 2030 fall below its revenue for that year. That would mean the pile is being worked down.

I am also wrong if Grid's backlog in euros falls for two quarters in a row before that date. A currency swing could cause that alone, and I will say so if it does, but I will still count it.

And I am wrong if Siemens Energy publishes a revised chart showing capacity meeting demand before fiscal 2030. Its own estimate is the foundation of this piece.
