Siemens Energy has published something I rarely see from an equipment maker: how far demand for large transformers runs ahead of the factories.

The chart appeared at its capital markets day in November 2025, a briefing where a company sets out its plans to investors. It covers what Siemens Energy calls all market players for large and medium power transformers in Europe and North America, not only itself.

On that chart, demand in fiscal 2025 ran about 40 per cent ahead of the whole industry's capacity. By fiscal 2030, after five more years of factory building, demand is still ahead, by about 10 per cent.

Most readers would call that a shortage coming to an end. On the chart's own terms, it is a shortage that leaves more orders waiting every single year.

By Siemens Energy's own count, the transformer shortage narrows to 2030, and the pile of orders waiting for it still grows.

<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a bath where the tap runs harder than the plughole can drain. The water rises.</p>
<p>So you widen the plughole, and over a few years you nearly double it. But someone keeps turning the tap up as well.</p>
<p>By the end, the tap runs only a little faster than the drain. The water rises more slowly than before, but it still rises, and it never falls. That is Siemens Energy's own picture of the transformer market to 2030.</p>

## What Siemens Energy makes for the grid

Siemens Energy is the German energy equipment group that Siemens separated and listed in 2020. Its fiscal year ends on 30 September, so fiscal 2025 is the year to 30 September 2025.

It reports four businesses: Gas Services for gas turbines, Siemens Gamesa for wind, Transformation of Industry, and Grid Technologies. This piece is about the last, and how it compares with the first.

**Power transformers.** The machine at the centre of a substation, which steps voltage down from the level used for long distance transport to one a site can use. Siemens Energy says one in five of those installed worldwide came from its factories.

**Switchgear.** The heavy equipment that makes and breaks high voltage connections safely, so one fault does not take down the grid around it.

**HVDC and substations.** High voltage direct current links carry bulk power over long distances with less loss. Siemens Energy says it built 30 per cent of those installed worldwide, and it also builds whole substations as projects.

In fiscal 2025, Grid Technologies had revenue of €11.3 billion, about 30 per cent from transformers and 15 per cent from switchgear. In the quarter to June 2026, Siemens Energy said transformers, including data centre projects, made the largest contribution to Grid's order growth.

## The chart that counts the whole industry

The slide is titled "Dynamic products market requiring further investments to strengthen our #1 position", a ranking Siemens Energy gives itself. Its subheading is plainer: "Market remains tight".

The chart measures transformers in gigavolt-amperes, GVA, a unit for how much power a transformer can carry. It sets industry capacity in fiscal 2023 at 100.

By my reading of the bars, industry capacity reaches about 115 in fiscal 2025, 170 in fiscal 2028 and 190 in fiscal 2030. Demand runs at roughly 165, 195 and 210. The slide labels only the gaps, about 40 per cent and about 10 per cent.

So the industry nearly doubles its capacity between fiscal 2023 and 2030, or adds about 60 per cent from today. Demand still finishes ahead.

Siemens Energy is doing its share. It says about €600 million spent on transformer and switchgear factories in fiscal 2023 to 2025 added about 20 per cent of transformer capacity. It plans about €2 billion more for transformers and switchgear by fiscal 2028. A €220 million expansion at Nuremberg, where transformers have been made since 1912, adds about 50 per cent there by 2028.

New floor space is slow to arrive. When Siemens Energy announced its first American large transformer plant, at Charlotte, North Carolina, in February 2024, it said production would begin in early 2026. In June 2025 Tim Holt, the board member responsible for the grid business, told Reuters early 2027.

## A narrower gap still adds to the pile

Here is the part the chart does not say out loud. If yearly demand stays above yearly output, the orders left unfilled at the end of each year are added to those already waiting.

A 40 per cent gap adds to the pile quickly. A 10 per cent gap adds slowly. Neither takes anything away from it, so the pile only stops growing when the gap reaches zero.

That reading is mine. It assumes the chart compares yearly demand with yearly output, which the slide does not state. Capacity may also count only factories inside the region, so imports could fill part of the gap.

It does not mean the wait in years gets longer. Faster factories can shorten the wait while the pile still grows. My claim is only about the pile.

Siemens Energy's own order book behaves the way the chart implies. Its backlog, meaning signed orders still to be built and shipped, was €33 billion at the end of fiscal 2024, €42 billion a year later and €51 billion in June 2026. In the nine months to June, the book-to-bill ratio, new orders divided by sales delivered, was about 1.9.

The fit is not exact. The chart covers transformers in Europe and North America, while the backlog is global and about 38 per cent HVDC and offshore projects, by my arithmetic from Siemens Energy's split. It is also in euros, so a weaker dollar shrinks American orders on paper. My tests below use Siemens Energy's global Grid figures as a stand-in for the chart, so one large HVDC order, or its absence, can move them either way.

## Grid against gas, inside one company

Siemens Energy's gas turbine and grid businesses sell to many of the same buyers, and report side by side.

In the nine months to June 2026, about 95 per cent of Grid Technologies' outside revenue came from new equipment. At Gas Services, 60 per cent came from servicing turbines already running.

Yet Grid earned more. Its profit margin before special items, profit before one-off costs such as restructuring as a share of revenue, was 18.3 per cent. Gas Services made 16.6 per cent, though it is still working through older turbine orders signed at lower prices.

My reading is that buyers are paying more for scarce grid equipment than for the turbines themselves. Siemens Energy credits Grid's gains to the "improved margin profile of the processed order backlog", meaning orders signed once the shortage was plain.

Grid's margin was 3.6 per cent in fiscal 2022. In November 2024, Siemens Energy's fiscal 2028 target for Grid was a reported margin of 13 to 15 per cent. By May 2026 it was guiding to 18 to 20 per cent for fiscal 2026 itself.

Siemens Gamesa, the wind business, shows the reverse. Its nine-month orders were less than half its revenue and its margin was just below zero. Siemens Energy puts the weak orders partly down to a comparison with two offshore orders worth over €3 billion a year earlier, so I treat it as a contrast, not proof.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Grid Technologies' orders for any full fiscal year before the end of fiscal 2030, on 30 September 2030, fall below its revenue for that year. That would mean the pile is being worked down.

I am also wrong if Grid's backlog in euros falls for two quarters in a row before that date. A currency swing could cause that alone, and I will say so if it does, but I will still count it.

And I am wrong if Siemens Energy publishes a revised chart showing capacity meeting demand before fiscal 2030. Its own estimate is the foundation of this piece.

So I watch Grid's orders against revenue each year, its backlog each quarter, Charlotte and Nuremberg's start dates, and any redrawn chart.
