Siemens Energy has published something I rarely see from an equipment maker: how far demand for large transformers runs ahead of every factory in Europe and North America, not only its own.

On the chart, from its capital markets day last November, demand ran about 40 per cent ahead of the whole industry's capacity in fiscal 2025. In fiscal 2030, after five more years of factory building, it is still about 10 per cent ahead.

Most readers would call that a shortage coming to an end. On the chart's own terms, it leaves more orders waiting every single year.

By Siemens Energy's own count, the transformer shortage narrows to 2030, and the pile of orders waiting for it still grows.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a bath where the tap runs harder than the plughole can drain. The water rises. So you widen the plughole, nearly doubling it over a few years, but someone keeps turning the tap up too.</p>
<p>By the end the tap runs only a little faster than the drain. The water rises more slowly, but it still rises, and it never falls. That is Siemens Energy's own picture of the transformer market to 2030.</p>
</details>

## The chart that counts the whole industry

A power transformer is the machine at the centre of a substation. It steps voltage down from the level used for long distance transport to one a site can use. A data centre cannot connect to the grid without one, and Siemens Energy says one in five installed worldwide came from its factories.

Its chart measures transformers in gigavolt-amperes, GVA, a unit for how much power a transformer can carry. By my reading of the bars, the industry nearly doubles its capacity over the years shown. Demand still finishes ahead.

New floor space is slow to arrive. Siemens Energy is adding about half again at Nuremberg by 2028. Its first American large transformer plant, at Charlotte, North Carolina, slipped from early 2026 to a year later.

Here is the part the chart does not say out loud. If yearly demand stays above yearly output, the orders left unfilled each year join those already waiting. A narrow gap adds to the pile more slowly than a wide one, but neither takes anything away. That reading is mine, and it assumes the chart compares yearly demand with yearly output.

## The order book behaves the same way

Siemens Energy's own order book fits the chart. The backlog of its grid business, Grid Technologies, meaning signed orders still to be built and shipped, has risen every year, to €51 billion in June 2026. In the nine months to June, Grid took in new orders nearly twice as fast as it delivered them.

The fit is not exact. Grid's backlog is global, and it includes long HVDC projects, high voltage direct current links that carry bulk power over long distances. But a pile that grows every year is what a gap that never closes looks like.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Grid Technologies takes fewer orders than its revenue in any full fiscal year before 30 September 2030. That would mean the pile is being worked down.

I am also wrong if Grid's backlog in euros falls for two quarters in a row before then, even if a currency swing is the cause. And I am wrong if Siemens Energy publishes a revised chart showing capacity meeting demand before fiscal 2030.
