Here is a claim I am willing to be wrong about in public. The AI material Ajinomoto sells is counted not in chips, but in area.

Ajinomoto is the Tokyo company that put monosodium glutamate on kitchen shelves. It also makes Ajinomoto Build-up Film, or ABF, an insulating film inside the base of high-performance processors.

That base is getting wider and taller every generation, and the film in each one rises with it.

Ajinomoto's film grows with the size of each AI package, and the time it takes to trust a rival keeps that growth with Ajinomoto.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Think of a lasagne. The pasta sheets keep each layer of sauce apart. Make the dish wider and add layers, and you need far more pasta, even though it is still one dish.</p>
<p>Now suppose only one shop makes sheets that survive your oven. A cheaper sheet must pass several test bakes before you trust it with guests. Ajinomoto makes the pasta.</p>
</details>

## Why the film grows faster than the chips

The base under a processor is the package substrate, a laminated circuit board that carries power and signals out to the rest of the computer. Build-up film is the insulating sheet laid between each layer of copper wiring in it.

Until recently, a high-performance package was about 70 millimetres square, with nine wiring layers. An AI package in 2026 is about 100 millimetres square, with eleven. From 2031 Ajinomoto projects about 120 millimetres and thirteen layers.

Film is laid across the whole board, once per layer. By my rough arithmetic, today's package needs about two and a half times the film of that older one.

Supply cannot simply catch up. A rival film must pass qualification, the testing a customer runs before trusting a new part, through laser drilling, plating and repeated heating without the board warping. Ajinomoto says the trust built over decades "serves as a barrier for new entrants." A third plant, in Gifu, is about six years from land to first film.

## What Ajinomoto's own numbers show

If area drives volume, the biggest boards should be taking over Ajinomoto's mix. They are.

Servers and networks, the high end, have risen to 70 per cent of its film volume, up from 40 per cent in fiscal 2017. And the business built around the film earns a profit margin of over half its sales.

A margin that stays that wide while volume grows is what a material customers dare not swap looks like.

## What would prove me wrong

Ajinomoto's annual results presentations test both halves, through fiscal 2029, the year to March 2030.

The area half fails if servers and networks fall below 70 per cent of its film volume in any of those years. The trust half fails if the film business's margin falls below 50 per cent in a year its sales still grow. Falling margin on rising sales is what a qualified, cheaper rival would look like.
