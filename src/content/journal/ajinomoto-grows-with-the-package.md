Here is a claim I am willing to be wrong about in public. The AI material Ajinomoto sells is not counted in chips, but in area.

Ajinomoto is the Tokyo company that put monosodium glutamate on kitchen shelves more than a century ago. It also makes Ajinomoto Build-up Film, or ABF, an insulating film used inside the base of high-performance processors.

In [the map of who makes an Nvidia B200](/journal/nobody-makes-a-b200-alone/), I placed that film close to the links with no second source. This piece goes one layer deeper, into why Ajinomoto's film volume grows faster than the number of chips.

The base under an AI chip is getting wider and taller every generation. Ajinomoto says the film used in each one rises with it.

Ajinomoto's film grows with the size of each AI package, and the time it takes to trust a rival keeps that growth with Ajinomoto, at high margins.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Think of a lasagne. The sheets of pasta keep each layer of sauce apart, so the dish holds together in the oven.</p>
<p>Make the dish wider and add more layers, and you need far more pasta, even though it is still one dish. Now suppose only one shop makes sheets that survive your oven. A cheaper sheet must pass several test bakes before you trust it with guests.</p>
<p>AI chip packages are lasagnes getting wider and taller every year. Ajinomoto makes the pasta, and nobody wants to risk the dinner on an untested sheet.</p>
</details>

## Two words you need

**Package substrate.** The laminated circuit board under a processor that carries power and signals out to the rest of the computer. Makers such as Ibiden in Japan and Unimicron in Taiwan build them, layer upon layer.

**Build-up film.** The insulating sheet laid between each layer of copper wiring in that board. Ajinomoto's version hardens permanently when heated, and is made from epoxy resin and fine mineral filler.

The film has to take a laser-drilled hole and have copper plated straight onto its surface. That is hard to do well, and Ajinomoto has done it for a long time.

Ajinomoto describes it as "a product of choice for nearly all high-performance CPUs", meaning the central processors in computers and servers. That is the company's own account of its position.

## Why the film grows faster than the chips

Ajinomoto's results presentation in May drew the trend as a picture of the board itself.

Until recently, a package for high-performance computing was about 70 millimetres square, with nine layers of wiring. An AI package in 2026 is about 100 millimetres square, with eleven layers. From 2031 Ajinomoto projects about 120 millimetres square and thirteen layers.

Film is laid across the whole board, once per layer. By my rough arithmetic, today's package needs about two and a half times the film of that older one. The package Ajinomoto projects needs more than four times as much.

That arithmetic is mine, but the direction is Ajinomoto's. Its latest integrated report says that as boards get larger with more layers, "the volume of ABF used is also rising along with this trend."

So a year with the same number of AI chips can still need much more film. Counting processors understates what the film business is really tracking.

## What Ajinomoto's own numbers show

If area drives volume, the biggest boards should be taking over Ajinomoto's mix. They are.

Ajinomoto publishes its film volume by application. Servers and networks, the high end, have risen to 70 per cent of volume, up from 40 per cent in fiscal 2017. Personal computers have shrunk to a small share.

The money moved with the mix. Sales in Functional Materials, the business built around the film, rose by almost a third last year.

Its business profit margin, Ajinomoto's main measure of profit as a share of sales, was over half. In fiscal 2018 it was about 30 per cent.

A margin that widens while volume grows is what a material customers dare not swap looks like in money.

## Why nobody switches

Rival films exist. Sekisui Chemical sells build-up films for the boards under server and network processors. LG Chem says it is developing one for AI server substrates.

The obstacle is qualification, the testing a customer runs before trusting a new part. A film must survive lamination, laser drilling, plating and repeated heating and cooling without the board warping or cracking.

Nobody publishes how long that takes. Ajinomoto describes its own cycle of sample, customer test and redevelopment, repeated until the customer approves. It says the trust built over decades "serves as a barrier for new entrants."

The cost of the film makes switching less likely, not more. By my arithmetic, Ajinomoto's whole film business sells less than half of one per cent of what Nvidia earns from data centres alone.

My inference is that saving money on the film is never worth risking a warped board under a far costlier processor.

## A third plant, years away

The growth has to come out of Ajinomoto's factories, and its timetable is slow by design.

Ajinomoto makes the film at two sites in Japan, Kawasaki and Gunma, and opened a new facility at Gunma last year. In May it announced it would buy land in Kani City, Gifu, for a third site.

By my count, that is about six years from choosing the land to the first film.

My inference is that until then, any extra film has to come from the existing sites. A rival, meanwhile, needs its own long qualification. The slowest part is time, and money cannot buy it back.

## What would prove me wrong

My claim has two parts, and Ajinomoto's annual results presentation tests both, through fiscal 2029, the year to March 2030.

The area part fails if servers and networks fall below 70 per cent of Ajinomoto's film volume in any of those years. Ajinomoto rounds the split to five points, so a flat reading at exactly that level passes.

The margin part fails if the Functional Materials business profit margin falls below 50 per cent in a year when its sales still grow. Falling margin on rising sales is what a qualified, cheaper rival would look like.

Ajinomoto discloses that margin only as rounded wording, "over 50%". If it stops doing so, the test rests on the volume split alone.
