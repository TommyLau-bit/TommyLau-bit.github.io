Here is a claim I am willing to be wrong about in public. The AI material Ajinomoto sells is not counted in chips, but in area.

Ajinomoto is the Tokyo company that put monosodium glutamate on kitchen shelves in 1909. It also makes Ajinomoto Build-up Film, or ABF, an insulating film used inside the base of high-performance processors.

In [the map of who makes an Nvidia B200](/journal/nobody-makes-a-b200-alone/), I placed that film close to the links with no second source. This piece goes one layer deeper, into why Ajinomoto's film volume grows faster than the number of chips.

The base under an AI chip is getting wider and taller every generation. Ajinomoto says the film used in each one rises with it.

Ajinomoto's film grows with the size of each AI package, and the time it takes to trust a rival keeps that growth with Ajinomoto, at high margins.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Think of a lasagne. The sheets of pasta keep each layer of sauce apart, so the dish holds together in the oven.</p>
<p>Make the dish wider and add more layers, and you need far more pasta, even though it is still one dish. Now suppose only one shop makes sheets that survive your oven. A cheaper sheet must pass several test bakes before you trust it with guests.</p>
<p>AI chip packages are lasagnes getting wider and taller every year. Ajinomoto makes the pasta, and nobody wants to risk the dinner on an untested sheet.</p>
</details>

## Two words you need

**Package substrate.** The laminated circuit board under a processor that carries power and signals out to the rest of the computer. Makers such as Ibiden in Japan and Unimicron in Taiwan build them, layer upon layer.

**Build-up film.** The insulating sheet laid between each layer of copper wiring in that board. Ajinomoto's version is a thermosetting film, which hardens permanently when heated, made from epoxy resin and fine mineral filler.

The film has to take a laser-drilled hole and copper plated straight onto its surface. Ajinomoto says it was first adopted by a major chipmaker in 1999.

Ajinomoto describes it as "a product of choice for nearly all high-performance CPUs", meaning central processors. Its investor guide goes further, claiming more than 95 per cent of the global market for insulating films for high-performance semiconductors. That is Ajinomoto's own estimate.

## Why the film grows faster than the chips

Ajinomoto's results presentation of 7 May 2026 drew the trend as a picture of the board itself. These sizes are the company's own estimates.

A package for high-performance computing up to 2023 was about 70 millimetres square, with nine layers of wiring. An AI package in 2026 is about 100 millimetres square, with eleven layers. From 2031 Ajinomoto projects about 120 millimetres square and thirteen layers.

Film is laid across the whole board, once per layer. By my rough arithmetic, area times layers gives about two and a half times the film per package today as in 2023. The 2031 package needs more than four times as much.

That arithmetic is mine, but the direction is Ajinomoto's. In its integrated report published in September 2026, it says that as boards get larger with more layers, "the volume of ABF used is also rising along with this trend."

So a year with the same number of AI chips can still need much more film. The count of processors understates what the film business is really tracking.

## What Ajinomoto's own numbers show

If area drives volume, the biggest boards should be taking over Ajinomoto's mix. They are.

Ajinomoto publishes its film volume by application. Servers and networks at the high end were 40 per cent of volume in fiscal 2017. By fiscal 2025, the year to March 2026, they were 70 per cent. Personal computers fell from 45 to 20 per cent.

Ajinomoto's own outlook puts servers and networks at 75 to 85 per cent by fiscal 2030. That is its forecast, not mine.

The money moved with the mix. Sales in Functional Materials, the business built around the film inside Ajinomoto's Healthcare and Others segment, rose 31 per cent in fiscal 2025, to ¥100.7 billion.

Its business profit margin, Ajinomoto's main measure of operating profit as a share of sales, was over 50 per cent. In fiscal 2018 it was about 30 per cent.

Ajinomoto Fine-Techno, the subsidiary that makes the film, reported ¥53.0 billion of operating profit on ¥98.4 billion of sales in fiscal 2025. In April to June 2026, Functional Materials sales rose 54 per cent and business profit 77 per cent on a year earlier.

Ajinomoto says the share of high-priced products in its electronic materials business has risen. A margin that widens while volume grows is what a material customers dare not swap looks like in money.

## Why nobody switches

Rival films exist. Sekisui Chemical sells build-up films for the boards under server and network processors. LG Chem says it is developing build-up film for AI server and high-performance computing substrates.

The obstacle is qualification, the testing a customer runs before trusting a new part. A film must survive lamination, laser drilling, plating and repeated heating and cooling without the board warping or cracking.

Nobody publishes how long a new build-up film takes to qualify. Ajinomoto describes its own cycle of sample, customer evaluation and redevelopment, repeated until the customer approves. It says the trust built with customers over more than 25 years "serves as a barrier for new entrants."

The cost of the film makes switching worse, not better. Ajinomoto's whole Functional Materials business sold about $670 million in fiscal 2025, at the ¥150 to the dollar it assumes. Nvidia alone reported $193.7 billion of data centre revenue in its year to January 2026.

By my arithmetic, that is under half of one per cent, and Ajinomoto's film also goes into PCs and other chipmakers' parts. My inference is that saving money on the film is never worth risking a warped board under a far costlier processor.

## The plant that opens in 2032

The growth has to come out of Ajinomoto's factories, and its timetable is slow by design.

Ajinomoto says its investment in expanding output towards 2030 totals ¥25 billion since 2023. It has two production sites, Kawasaki and Gunma, and a new facility at Gunma started operating in 2025.

On 7 May 2026 it announced it would buy land in Kani City, Gifu, for a third site, for about ¥1.2 billion. Construction is planned from 2028 and operation in fiscal 2032, to meet demand after 2030.

That is about six years from choosing the land to the first film. My inference is that until then, any extra film has to come from the existing sites. A rival, meanwhile, needs its own qualification time.

This journal keeps finding the same pattern, from transformers to TSMC's packaging lines. The slowest part is time, and money cannot buy it back.

As elsewhere on this site, the scarce part is not the chip doing the maths but what surrounds it.

## What would prove me wrong

My claim has two parts, and Ajinomoto's annual results presentation tests both, for its Functional Materials business, through fiscal 2029, the year to March 2030.

The area part fails if servers and networks fall below 70 per cent of Ajinomoto's film volume in any of those years. Ajinomoto rounds the split to five points, so a flat 70 per cent passes, as it did from fiscal 2024 to 2025.

The margin part fails if the Functional Materials business profit margin falls below 50 per cent in a year when its sales still grow. Falling margin on rising sales is what a qualified, cheaper rival would look like.

Ajinomoto discloses that margin only as rounded wording, "over 50%". If it stops printing it, the test falls back on the volume split alone.

Two things could blur the test. Functional Materials includes some products other than film, such as adhesives. And Ajinomoto plans to merge Ajinomoto Fine-Techno into the parent on 1 April 2027, with a board vote planned for 26 November 2026, so its separate accounts would stop.

So I watch four things. The volume split by application each May. The Functional Materials margin each year. Whether Gifu construction starts in 2028. And whether Sekisui or LG Chem announce their film in AI server substrates.
