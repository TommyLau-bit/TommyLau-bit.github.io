Here is a claim I am willing to be wrong about in public. Schneider Electric's growth from AI is not mainly a story of selling more breakers and switches.

Schneider, the French electrical group, sells catalogue parts and bigger systems it builds and tests before delivery. For a year the systems have grown faster, and data centres led them in the latest quarter.

What those buyers want is the finished article: power rooms and cooling plants assembled in a factory, then shipped ready to connect.

Schneider Electric is selling AI data centres the finished system, because the hours on site are now scarcer than the parts.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Nobody builds a fridge in their own kitchen. The compressor, pipes and thermostat are joined and tested in a factory, and the fridge arrives as one sealed box.</p>
<p>Buy those parts loose and you need an engineer to fit them, and every engineer in town is booked for months. The boxed fridge is cold the evening it arrives. Schneider is selling AI data centres more fridges and fewer loose parts.</p>
</details>

## Loose parts or a finished room

Schneider sorts its sales into three groups. Products are catalogue items such as circuit breakers and switches, which electricians fit on site. Software and services are design tools and the engineers who maintain the kit.

Systems, now about a third of sales, are the bigger offers. For data centres that means prefabricated modules, power or cooling rooms built inside a factory, wired and tested, then shipped in one piece. It also means UPS, uninterruptible power supplies, large battery systems that carry a hall through a dip in the grid.

An AI hall is not finished when its equipment arrives. Every panel and battery string still has to be installed and commissioned, which means tested under power before it carries a real load.

That takes electricians, and every new AI campus draws on the same pool as homes, factories and the grid itself. The pool does not grow because one industry needs it to. Prefabrication does not abolish the wait, but it moves part of it from the building site into the factory.

## The tell in Schneider's own results

If buyers wanted more of the same parts, Products would grow fastest. It does not. In the second quarter of 2026, Systems grew 28 per cent and Products 13 per cent, leaving out currency moves and acquisitions.

The shift even costs Schneider something. Its half year results blame the mix for pulling down its gross margin, the share of each sale left after the cost of making it. I read that as a telling choice: a thinner cut on each sale, in return for delivering more of the work already done.

Its executives say why. Frédéric Godemel, who runs its energy business, described "tension on the number of people to commission product and install them".

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if Schneider's growth stops arriving as systems. Systems was 34 per cent of its sales in 2025.

If that share falls below 34 per cent in 2026 or 2027, while Schneider still says its data centre sales grow by double digits, the claim fails. It also fails if catalogue Products outgrow Systems, leaving out currency moves and acquisitions, for two quarters running while data centres remain its main driver.
