<a id="nuclear-for-ai"></a>

The earliest new nuclear power that Microsoft has bought for AI will come from a reactor that shut in 2019. Constellation, America's largest nuclear plant owner, is bringing the former Three Mile Island Unit 1 in Pennsylvania back to life, renamed Crane, and says it will run again in the second half of 2027.

Meanwhile the new reactors have nearly everything a press release needs. TerraPower, the reactor company founded by Bill Gates, has a construction permit in Wyoming. Oklo, a start-up that plans to sell power from its own small reactors, has broken ground in Idaho and signed Meta. Kairos Power, a California reactor developer, has a contract to sell power for Google. Yet on their own dates, none of them will send meaningful power to an American grid before the 835 megawatts of a reactor built in the 1970s.

That is the claim of this piece. Nuclear power for AI arrives in the order of what each project already holds, not in the order of how advanced its reactor is. A restart or an upgrade of an existing reactor keeps a licensed site, a place on the grid and ordinary fuel. Most new designs need a richer fuel that America makes on one small demonstration line, so they wait.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>The analogy</summary>
<p>You need a cooker working this month. The old one in the garage still has its socket in the wall and runs on gas from any supplier. It needs a safety check and a new hose, and then it cooks. Fitting a bigger burner to the cooker you already use, while the kitchen is shut for cleaning, gets you a little more heat for very little money.</p>
<p>The new model is better, but it runs on cartridges that one small shop fills a few at a time, and the council keeps the only spare box. The shop will not build a factory until enough new cookers are sold, and nobody buys a cooker without cartridges. So the old cooker makes dinner for years before the new one does. One catch: a quarter of the gas for the old cooker has come from a supplier you have just banned.</p>
</details>

## Why do AI companies want nuclear power at all?

Because a nuclear plant runs almost every hour of the year without burning anything, and an AI data centre needs power every hour of the year.

The measure is the **capacity factor**: what a plant actually produced in a year, as a share of running flat out all year. America's nuclear plants reached 91.1 per cent in 2025, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA), the government's energy statistics office. Efficient combined-cycle gas plants managed 58.7 per cent, wind 33.8 per cent and large solar farms 24.0 per cent.

That matters most to the companies that promised clean power around the clock. Google aims by 2030 to match every hour of electricity it uses with carbon-free power on the same grid in the same hour, and in 2024 it reached 66 per cent. Nuclear is the only carbon-free source that runs nearly every hour at the scale of many gigawatts.

America already has 94 reactors at 54 plants, with about 98 gigawatts, which made 17.7 per cent of its electricity in 2025 (my division of EIA figures).

## What does "new" nuclear power for AI actually mean?

One of three things, arriving in this order: restarting a closed reactor, squeezing more power out of a running one, or building a new one.

A **restart** brings back a closed reactor. Three are under way: Crane (835 MW) in Pennsylvania, Palisades (about 800 MW) in Michigan and Duane Arnold (615 MW) in Iowa. An **uprate** lets a running reactor make more heat, and so more power, under a licence change from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), the federal regulator. A **new build** starts from bare ground: a large reactor of about 1,100 MW, a **small modular reactor** (SMR) of up to about 300 MW, or an **advanced reactor** with a different coolant or fuel, most of which need HALEU.

**HALEU**, high-assay low-enriched uranium, is the fuel that divides the field. Natural uranium is only about 0.7 per cent uranium-235, the atom that splits. Today's reactors burn fuel concentrated to about 5 per cent; HALEU is concentrated to just under 20 per cent, which lets a small or fast-neutron core run for years. It takes roughly five times the enrichment work per kilogram of ordinary 5 per cent fuel.

## What does a nuclear project need before it can sell a kilowatt-hour?

Four things, each with its own clock: a place on the grid, a reactor that works, a licence to run it, and fuel. The slowest sets the date.

The **grid place** is the right to connect to the high-voltage network at a set size; a new one means joining a queue while the grid operator studies what must be built. The NRC must approve the design, site, operators and security plan. And fuel must be mined, enriched, made into assemblies and delivered.

## How does a closed reactor come back to life?

Through a regulatory path that did not exist until 2023, a few years of repair and hiring, and about US$1.6 billion in Crane's case.

<a id="constellation-restarts-the-reactor"></a>

Crane shut on 20 September 2019 because it could not earn enough selling power into the market. It was placed in **SAFSTOR**, a form of decommissioning in which a closed plant is kept intact and guarded while its radioactivity decays. Once an owner moves to active dismantling, the reactor vessel and its internals are cut up and removed, and nothing remains to restart. Apart from the three, no closed American reactor has a restart application at the NRC.

Once a reactor has shut for good, its licence no longer allows it to run. Holtec, a private nuclear company, built a way back for Palisades from February 2023: an exemption from that rule, licence amendments and an environmental assessment, approved together. For Palisades it took about 30 months, to approval on 24 July 2025.

Crane is following the same path, about a year behind. Constellation filed in November 2024, and an NRC schedule letter of January 2026 targets a decision by 4 March 2027.

The money is modest by nuclear standards. Constellation says it has invested about US$1.6 billion. Microsoft buys the output under a 20-year contract signed in September 2024. That is roughly US$1,900 per kilowatt, on my arithmetic; a new large reactor costs several times as much.

## Why did Crane have to borrow a place on the grid?

Because its own place expired a year after it closed, and a fresh one might not have been fully ready until the end of 2030 or later.

The grid here is run by **PJM**, the operator of the high-voltage network and power market. Under PJM's tariff, a closed plant's **capacity interconnection rights**, its right to put a set number of megawatts into the network, end one year after it shuts. Crane's lapsed in 2020.

So Crane applied again like a new plant. PJM's first study, in October 2025, found its full output could be delivered only once new transmission was built, some due as late as December 2030.

So Constellation borrowed a plug from its own fleet. On 1 June 2026 the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), which regulates interstate power markets, let it move 760 MW of rights from Eddystone Units 3 and 4, two of its oil and gas units near Philadelphia, to Crane. FERC said this could "potentially" let Crane run fully before the end of 2030, and PJM's studies still decide.

## If holding a grid place is enough, why is Palisades late?

Because a grid place makes a restart possible, not quick. Palisades kept its place, and its reactor work set the pace instead.

<a id="palisades-waits-on-the-reactor"></a>

Palisades sits on the grid of **MISO**, the operator for much of the Midwest. There a closed plant can stay "suspended", keeping its connection, for at most 36 months in any five years; FERC extended Palisades' by 22 months, to 1 March 2027. It was reconnected to its 345 kilovolt switchyard on 11 September 2026. The reactor was harder. Its **steam generators**, the heat exchangers that boil water into steam, had worn tubes, which Holtec sleeved rather than replace.

Loading fuel began on 30 August 2026. Then a fuel assembly tilted about 30 degrees after it was placed, and loading stopped. Recovering it took a month and an emergency licence amendment. Holtec has given no new restart date.

The deadline is commercial. Palisades sells to two power cooperatives under 28-year contracts, and if it is not in service by March 2027, either side may end them.

The third restart shows the same lesson the other way round. NextEra Energy kept Duane Arnold's grid place through a FERC waiver of August 2025, for up to 619 MW provided the plant runs by 31 December 2029. Google buys most of the output under a 25-year contract, and CIPCO, an Iowa power cooperative, the rest on the same terms. Its harder job is physical: a storm in August 2020 wrecked its cooling towers, so NextEra must build new ones. Its date is the first quarter of 2029 at the latest.

## What is an uprate, and why is it the cheapest new nuclear megawatt?

An uprate is a licence change that lets a running reactor make more heat, on a site that already has everything else, so it costs a fraction of a new plant per kilowatt.

<a id="constellation-squeezes-the-reactors"></a>

The NRC sorts uprates by size. A **measurement uncertainty recapture** uprate adds less than 2 per cent through more accurate meters. A **stretch** uprate adds up to about 7 per cent, mostly by resetting instruments. An **extended** uprate adds up to 20 per cent, and needs new turbines, generators, pumps or transformers.

The NRC has approved 172 uprates since 1977, adding 8,030 MW. Then the work stopped: the last came in November 2021. AI buyers have restarted it. The NRC's list of expected applications, updated on 8 September 2026, shows 31 uprates from the fourth quarter of 2026 to 2032, adding about 2,421 MW. Nineteen are extended uprates, and twelve are Constellation's.

The deals behind them are specific. On 6 October 2026 Google agreed a 20-year contract for 890 MW of uprates at eleven Constellation reactors, which Constellation will invest more than US$4.3 billion to build. Amazon has signed for about 190 MW at Calvert Cliffs in Maryland, and Meta for 30 MW at Clinton in Illinois and 433 MW at three plants of Vistra, a Texas power company. Google has signed for about 96 MW in Georgia, still awaiting state approval. Together that is about 1,640 MW, on my sum.

Google's 890 MW for US$4.3 billion is about US$4,800 per kilowatt. The two new reactors at Vogtle in Georgia cost their owners roughly US$14,000 per kilowatt, after years of overruns (my arithmetic, from reported totals).

The catches are timing and equipment. Heavy work can be done only while a reactor is shut to reload fuel, every 18 to 24 months, so the megawatts arrive in steps: Constellation's first by 2028, Google's last before the end of 2032. Extended uprates need the same transformers and generators as everyone else, so they join [the equipment queue](/journal/the-equipment-queue/). And none of these AI-funded uprates had reached the NRC by September 2026.

## Does the nuclear power actually go to the data centre?

Almost never directly. On the PJM and MISO grids, every binding nuclear deal for AI sells power into the grid, and the data centre draws from the grid like any other customer.

**Behind the meter**, or co-located, a data centre beside the plant takes power before it reaches the grid. **In front of the meter**, the plant sells into the grid and the buyer signs a **power purchase agreement** (PPA): a long contract to pay a set price for a set amount of the plant's power.

The first big co-location deal hit a wall. In November 2024 FERC rejected a change that would have raised the load co-located with Talen Energy's Susquehanna plant for Amazon from 300 to 480 MW. So the deal moved in front of the meter: up to 1,920 MW through 2042, delivered through the grid. In an order of 18 December 2025 FERC required a co-located data centre in PJM to take one of three transmission services; a plant adding co-located load must now be studied, pay for any grid upgrades, and wait for them.

The exception is in Texas, on ERCOT's separate grid, where Vistra has a binding 20-year contract for 1,200 MW from Comanche Peak, with a campus planned beside the plant.

## Who has signed what, and how binding is it?

The binding contracts sit on reactors that already exist. The deals with new reactors are mostly funding, options and targets.

<a id="oklo-sells-the-electricity"></a>

Long, binding power purchase agreements on existing American reactors and their uprates cover about 9,880 MW (my sum of the companies' figures). The new-reactor side reads differently. Meta has funded development of two TerraPower reactors, up to 690 MW and as early as 2032. Oklo's 1.2 GW campus for Meta in Ohio rests on a framework under which Meta prepays for future power; Oklo says it is not a power purchase agreement. Google has a framework with Kairos Power for 500 MW by 2035, and Amazon a target of more than 5 GW from X-energy by 2039.

<a id="kairos-signs-before-it-switches-on"></a>

The one binding contract on a new reactor is small. On 18 August 2025 TVA, the federal power company for Tennessee, agreed to buy up to 50 MW from Kairos Power's Hermes 2 at Oak Ridge from 2030, for Google. Hermes 2's permit covers test reactors of 35 thermal megawatts each, and at a third efficiency even two of them cannot make 50 megawatts of electricity, so the contract implies a larger plant the NRC has not yet been asked to license. Its fuel source is not yet named.

The split follows the clocks. A firm 20-year price needs a reactor with a licence, a grid place and fuel; for one that does not yet exist, a buyer pays for development, not power.

## What about new reactors that burn ordinary fuel?

They avoid the fuel problem but not the other three clocks, and none of the commercial designs will send power to an American grid before 2030.

<a id="darlington-builds-on-the-old-plug"></a>

The closest is in Canada. Ontario Power Generation is building the first of four **BWRX-300s**, a 300 MW boiling-water small reactor from GE Vernova and Hitachi, on its licensed Darlington nuclear site. It burns ordinary fuel and targets grid connection by the end of 2030. The first unit, with shared systems, is C$7.7 billion, about C$25,700 per kilowatt (my arithmetic).

## Why do the advanced reactors wait on fuel?

Because most of them need HALEU, and America makes it on one line of sixteen centrifuges whose government contract has ended.

<a id="terrapower-waits-on-the-fuel"></a>

**Enrichment** concentrates uranium-235 by spinning uranium gas in fast centrifuges; a linked line of them is a **cascade**. The only American cascade to have made HALEU belongs to Centrus Energy, in Piketon, Ohio, with sixteen machines. By June 2026 it had made more than 1,900 kg of HALEU as gas, about 1.3 tonnes of uranium, in all its years of running (my conversion). Its contracted production finished in mid-June 2026, and the Energy Department does not currently intend to order more from it. Centrus's lease ends on 1 October 2027.

What is coming is planned, not built. Centrus's new government order, signed on 1 July 2026, requires one tonne of uranium as HALEU by July 2032. The first new centrifuge left its Tennessee factory on 29 October 2026. New capacity should start in Ohio in 2029, in a build-out that includes 12 tonnes a year of HALEU. Against that, the Energy Department expects "more than 40 metric tons" of HALEU to be needed by 2030. Its own stock should reach about 21 tonnes by 2028. It has conditionally allocated some in three rounds since April 2025, to NASA and eight others, without publishing anyone's tonnage.

## Is the old fleet's own fuel safe?

Mostly, but it has a deadline. A quarter of the enrichment America's reactors bought in 2025 came from Russia, and that supply runs out of road at the end of 2027.

<a id="eunice-cannot-fill-the-gap"></a>

Enrichment is measured in **separative work units** (SWU), the effort of enriching. In 2025 American reactor owners bought 12.71 million SWU, of which 3.28 million, 25.8 per cent, came from Russia, according to the EIA. America's ban on Russian enriched uranium allows waivers that all end by 1 January 2028, and Russia's own export curb runs to 31 December 2027.

The replacements arrive slowly. Urenco runs America's only commercial enrichment plant, at Eunice in New Mexico, with about 4.3 million SWU a year, adding 700,000 by spring 2027. Orano plans 4 million SWU at Oak Ridge in the early 2030s. There is a cushion: owners held 51.8 million pounds of already enriched uranium at the end of 2025 against 40.9 million pounds loaded that year. It is a squeeze on price and timing, not a wall like HALEU's.

Much of the raw uranium is not yet bought. At the end of 2025, contracts covered at most 174 million of the 360 million pounds of uranium concentrate that owners expect to need from 2026 to 2035, the EIA reports. The unsigned share is 6 per cent for 2027, 40 per cent for 2030 and 91 per cent for 2033. Utilities buy in steps, years ahead, so this is a buying schedule, not a shortage.

<a id="the-box-stops-at-five"></a>

One more limit applies to both lanes: the shipping container. The standard package for enriched uranium gas is certified only up to 5 per cent, and the one package approved for HALEU had not been manufactured as of April 2026, according to the NRC.

## So what reaches the grid by 2030?

Mostly old reactors. On the companies' own dates, about 2,250 MW of restarts and roughly 1,640 MW of AI-funded uprates should arrive in steps from 2027 to 2034, all on ordinary fuel. New reactors on HALEU add at most 470 MW by the end of 2030, if every one runs to schedule.

## So who holds the scarce thing, and what should an investor watch?

The scarce things are licensed sites with grid places, and enrichment capacity outside Russia. Few companies hold either, and money alone cannot build either quickly.

**The owners of the existing fleet** hold the first. Constellation, Vistra, NextEra, Talen and Holtec own reactors that buyers will pay to keep, restart or enlarge under 20-year contracts. **The enrichers**, Urenco, Orano and Centrus, hold the second, and their constraint is time. **The new reactor developers** hold permits, sites and option-like contracts, and need fuel; for them the date to watch is the first tonne of HALEU delivered for a commercial core.

The signals to watch are dated. Crane's first power in the second half of 2027, and whether Palisades meets its March 2027 contract deadline. The first AI-funded extended uprate filed at the NRC. Whether Centrus gets the longer Piketon lease the Energy Department says it will work to agree by 31 December 2026. Whether its 12 tonnes a year of HALEU becomes a contract, and whether ASP Isotopes enriches any uranium. And how much Russian enrichment still arrives as both deadlines fall at the end of 2027.

## What would prove me wrong

Two tests break the claim. If reactors fuelled with HALEU together send electricity to American grids from 100 MW or more of capacity by 31 December 2030, fuel was not the gate. If fewer than two of Crane, Palisades and Duane Arnold have delivered power to the grid by 31 December 2029, an existing site is not the fast lane.

Some events would move me before either test. Centrus contracting its 12 tonne a year build-out with firm customers, or ASP Isotopes enriching its first uranium-235, would start a commercial HALEU supply. So would a large Energy Department HALEU allocation beyond first cores. And a second restart stalling as Palisades has would weaken the first limb.

Three things would not move me. More signings with new reactor developers: a framework or option is not a delivery contract. More criticalities: a zero-power physics test proves a reactor, not a fuel supply. And Darlington: it burns ordinary fuel, and if it runs on time it supports the claim.
