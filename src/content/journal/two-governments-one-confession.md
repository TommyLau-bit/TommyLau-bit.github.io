There are two ways to find out what somebody thinks is scarce. You can ask them, or you can watch what they do about it.

The second is more reliable, because the first is a statement and the second is a cost.

Two governments in this region have spent the last few years responding to the same pressure, which is computing demand arriving faster than the grid was built for. They have responded in opposite ways. One spent to build more. The other decides who is allowed any. That pair is the strongest single piece of evidence I have for the argument I have been making all year.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a street where the water pressure has become a problem. One household has a garden and some money, so they drill their own well and lay their own pipe.</p>
<p>Next door is a block of flats with no land. They cannot make more water, so they put someone in charge of the tap, give each flat a set amount, and hand the largest share to whoever wastes the least.</p>
<p>The two households did completely different things. Neither would have bothered if water were plentiful. You learn more from watching them than from anything either one says about the supply.</p>
</details>

## Malaysia could make more, so it did

Tenaga Nasional has committed RM43 billion, about US$10.8 billion, to modernise the grid specifically for data centre demand. Its Green Lane Pathway cut the wait for a new connection from 36 months to 12, and had delivered 33 projects under that scheme by March 2026.

The detail that matters is what the money was aimed at. That is a connection programme. It buys wires, substations and a faster approval process. It does not buy power stations.

A utility that believed it was short of electricity would be building generation. Where an organisation spends is a more honest account of its problem than anything it says in a presentation, because spending costs something and saying does not. Tenaga's capital plan is a confession, and what it confesses is that getting electricity to the site, rather than producing it, was the thing stopping projects.

## Singapore cannot make more, so it rations

Singapore has almost no land and imports most of its energy. It froze new data centre approvals outright between 2019 and 2022. Peak demand is projected to grow 3.7 to 5.7 per cent a year, reaching somewhere between 10.1 and 11.8 gigawatts by 2030.

Its answer has been to release capacity in small controlled batches with conditions attached. A roughly 20 hectare low carbon data centre park on Jurong Island is sized to 700 megawatts. The capacity award requires a power efficiency score of 1.25 or better, which means the megawatts go to whoever wastes the fewest of them.

**Nobody rations something that is plentiful.** Singapore is allocating connection by efficiency because it cannot allocate it by supply, and a queue with entry conditions tells you what the regulator thinks the binding resource is.

## Why two opposite policies are one piece of evidence

This is the part I find genuinely useful, and it is an argument about evidence rather than about power.

If one country were doing this, you could explain it locally. A single utility's capital plan can be politics, or an accounting cycle, or a chief executive with a preference. One data point supports several stories.

Two jurisdictions with different politics, different land, different energy positions and different tools, arriving at the same operational conclusion by opposite routes, is much harder to explain away. One is spending to relieve a scarcity. The other is rationing access to it. Those are the only two things anyone can do about a scarce thing, and both are being done, next door to each other, about the same thing.

The thing is not electricity. It is the connection.

## The statistic that argues with itself

There is a number in the middle of this that I keep returning to.

Inside the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone, installed capacity was reported at 3,885 MVA in December 2025, against demand near 1,272 megawatts. The published utilisation figure is **72.76 per cent**.

Those two numbers divide to about 33 per cent.

So the published rate cannot be measured against total installed capacity. The only reading I can find that makes all three numbers consistent is that 72.76 per cent is measured against capacity actually energised, while the 3,885 headline includes substations that are approved but not yet commissioned.

If that is right, then an official statistic is already using the distinction that the rest of the conversation refuses to make. Announced capacity and energised capacity are being treated as two different quantities inside one government release, while developer disclosure and most commentary still treat them as one number.

I want to be careful here, because I am relying on a reconciliation I cannot verify from the published sources. It is the only arithmetic I can make work, and it may still be wrong. If the real explanation is different I would like to know. That would cost me this example, but not the argument.

## What would prove me wrong

The confession reading is the part to attack, so here is how I would attack it.

I would be wrong about Malaysia if that spending turns out to be ordinary grid renewal that was going to happen anyway and was relabelled for the moment. The test is whether it is disproportionately weighted towards connection assets rather than generation, which is checkable in the capital plan.

I would be wrong about Singapore if the efficiency ceiling is mainly climate policy rather than capacity policy. The test is what happens to the ceiling when capacity gets tight. A capacity instrument moves with scarcity. A climate instrument moves on a fixed schedule.

And I would be wrong about the pattern itself if a third market with a genuinely constrained grid does neither of these things, because then two cases are a coincidence rather than a rule.

So I watch the Green Lane project count beyond 33, the composition of the Singapore capacity award, because who wins under a strict efficiency ceiling tells you what the regulator believes is actually buildable, and whether the Johor utilisation figure is ever restated or explained.
