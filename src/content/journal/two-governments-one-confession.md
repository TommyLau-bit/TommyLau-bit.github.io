There are two ways to find out what somebody thinks is scarce. You can ask them, or you can watch what they do about it.

The second is more reliable, because a statement is free and an action has a cost.

Malaysia and Singapore have faced the same pressure, computing demand arriving faster than the grid was built for. One spent to build more connection. The other decides who is allowed any.

Opposite policies, one confession: the connection is the scarce thing.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Picture a street where the water pressure has become a problem. One household has a garden, so it drills its own well and lays its own pipe.</p>
<p>Next door, a block of flats has no land. It puts someone in charge of the tap and gives the largest share to whoever wastes least. Neither would have bothered if water were plentiful.</p>
</details>

## Malaysia builds, Singapore rations

**Malaysia could make more, so it did.** Tenaga Nasional, the national utility, aimed its data centre spending at connection: wires, substations and a faster approval process. It did not buy power stations. A utility short of electricity would be building generation. Where an organisation spends is a more honest account of its problem than anything it says in a presentation.

**Singapore cannot make more, so it rations.** It has almost no land and imports most of its energy, and peak demand is projected to climb every year to 2030. It froze new data centre approvals for about three years, then released capacity in small batches with conditions attached.

One case could be local politics or an accounting cycle. Two jurisdictions with different land, energy and tools reaching the same conclusion by opposite routes is much harder to explain away. Spending to relieve a scarcity and rationing access to it are the only two things anyone can do about a scarce thing.

## What each government actually did

Tenaga's Green Lane Pathway cut the wait for a new connection from 36 months to 12, and had delivered 33 projects by March 2026. You do not build that programme unless connection time was what stopped projects.

Singapore's capacity award requires a power efficiency score of 1.25 or better, so the megawatts go to whoever wastes the fewest of them. A queue with entry conditions tells you what the regulator thinks the binding resource is.

**Nobody rations something that is plentiful.**

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong about Malaysia if its spending turns out to be ordinary renewal, weighted towards generation rather than connection. I am wrong about Singapore if its efficiency ceiling moves on a fixed schedule rather than with scarcity, which would make it climate policy.

And I am wrong about the pattern if a third constrained market does neither.
