I have written twice now that the thing holding back AI is not the chip. It is the wait to [plug into the electricity grid](/journal/the-queue-not-the-chip/).

The obvious objection: if the queue is the problem, do not join it. Build your own power station next to the building, burn gas, and connect to nothing.

If that works at scale, most of what I have written is a two year story. So here is my honest attempt to argue against myself.

The escape route from the queue is itself a queue.

<details class="analogy">
<summary>Explain it like I don't work in finance</summary>
<p>Imagine a restaurant that cannot get a big enough water pipe from the council, with a long wait for the new one. The obvious answer is to drill your own well.</p>
<p>Then you ring the drilling company. They are booked for four years, because every restaurant on the street had the same idea. The well is a real solution. It is just not a fast one.</p>
</details>

## Why on-site power relocates the problem

Generating on site is called going behind the meter, meaning you make power on your own side of the utility's meter and never ask to draw from the public network. It genuinely removes the interconnection study and the place in the queue.

But a gas turbine does not connect itself to your building with an extension lead. It still needs transformers to step the voltage down, and switchgear, the heavy equipment that makes and breaks connections safely.

Those are the same components, from the same constrained supply chain, that make the grid connection slow. You have removed the utility. You have not removed the equipment.

Supply cannot catch up quickly either. Turbine makers are expanding, but over years, and running a power station brings fuel, permits and maintenance crews, none of them instant.

## What the turbine makers' order books say

Look at who makes the machines. GE Vernova has **116 gigawatts under contract**, part firm orders and part slot reservations, places held in a line to be manufactured. Its capacity is described as mostly sold out through 2030. Siemens Energy reports **95 gigawatts** on a similar mix.

Nobody reserves a factory slot years out for a power station they do not need. That backlog measures how badly the ordinary connection route is failing people.

## What would prove me wrong

I am wrong if turbine lead times fall while the backlog is still being worked through. I am also wrong if large sites energise on their own generation faster than comparable sites get a grid connection, the direct head to head test.

And I am wrong if transformers and switchgear, which sit under both routes, stop being the constraint.
