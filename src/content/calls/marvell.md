Marvell's shares closed at US$284.68 on 7 October 2026. On 4 February they closed at US$73.73, the lowest of the past year. In between, Nvidia bought a stake, Google signed a wider custom chip agreement with a warrant that vests on up to US$120 billion of revenue, and Marvell raised its outlook again and again. The latest raise came at its Investor Day in New York on 6 October.

My piece of 28 September argued something narrower. Marvell is paid every time AI chips talk, through the signal processor inside each optical plug, whoever made the chips. The results behind that piece support it, and so does the Investor Day. Interconnect is Marvell's largest data centre business today, and it is still the largest line in management's plan for fiscal 2031.

The question for the shares is how much of that plan the price already pays for. On my numbers, not much of what comes after fiscal 2028.

## The thesis

Go back to the torches from the September piece. Shout to someone three streets away and the words smear into noise. Flashes of light carry, but someone at each end must turn words into flashes and back again, without a mistake. Those two translators do not care who is talking. More talkers, talking faster, need more of them.

**Marvell is one of those translators.** It bought the job in April 2021, when it completed its purchase of Inphi. Its optical DSPs sit inside the transceivers at each end of a fibre, cleaning up a signal that carries two bits per pulse. Around them it sells the amplifiers and drivers beside the laser, the modules that link data centres, and the Ethernet switch chips the plugs slot into.

**The numbers say the toll is still the engine.** Data centre revenue was US$2.17 billion in the quarter to 1 August 2026, 79 per cent of the total and up 46 per cent on a year earlier. In May, Marvell said it expected interconnect, the largest part of the data centre business, to grow more than 70 per cent in fiscal 2027, the year to January 2027. In March it had guided custom chips to grow more than 20 per cent, from US$1.5 billion in fiscal 2026.

**The Investor Day kept the toll at the centre.** Marvell raised its fiscal 2028 revenue outlook to about US$20 billion, from about US$18 billion, which is 67 per cent growth. It put data centre revenue at about US$18 billion that year, a target it had set for fiscal 2029. For fiscal 2031 it set a revenue target of US$70 billion to US$90 billion. At the US$80 billion midpoint, interconnect is about US$37.5 billion, custom about US$30 billion, switching and storage about US$10 billion, and the rest about US$2.5 billion.

Marvell does not report interconnect in dollars. So I split data centre revenue myself, using what it does disclose: US$1.5 billion of custom revenue and more than US$300 million of switching in fiscal 2026. That leaves about US$4.3 billion for interconnect, storage and the rest. On my split, about three quarters of fiscal 2027's data centre growth comes from that remainder and only about a seventh from custom. In fiscal 2028 the remainder still supplies about two thirds.

Custom's turn comes in fiscal 2029. Marvell now targets more than US$12 billion of custom revenue that year, up from a target of US$10 billion, and more than three times fiscal 2028.

**The plug is staying, for now.** My piece said I was wrong if the translation moved off the plug, into linear optics or co-packaged optics. On the August call Matt Murphy, the chief executive, said pluggable modules remain the main form factor for scale-out networks and that he did not expect that to change. 800G demand remains strong and the 1.6T generation is ramping rapidly.

The twist is scale-up, the links inside one large AI system. There, Marvell expects optics to move close to the chip, as near-packaged or co-packaged parts. It bought Celestial AI for US$3.5 billion in February to make them, and forecasts a US$500 million annualised run rate from it in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2028. At the Investor Day it put interconnect revenue growth at about 65 per cent a year from fiscal 2026 to 2031, behind only custom at about 80 per cent. So where the translation does leave the plug, Marvell is so far one of the companies taking it.

**Where I was wrong in September.** My piece said: "On the same call, Marvell said it would make about $1 billion of capacity prepayments to suppliers in fiscal 2027, to secure supply of 1.6T optical DSPs and switch chips." The call was the one of 27 August, and Dan Durn, the finance chief, did repeat the US$1 billion plan on it. But neither he nor anyone else on that call or the May call, when the plan was first given, tied the money to optical DSPs or switch chips.

Durn said the prepayments back the design wins Marvell has secured and will be set against future material purchases. The 10-Q for the quarter to 1 August describes "capacity reservation arrangements with certain foundries and partners". The 10-K's commitments note names foundries and test and assembly partners. The balance sheet line, prepayments on supply capacity reservation agreements, rose from US$278.8 million to US$487.0 million in six months.

So the prepayments show a company buying capacity ahead of demand across the whole business, in a year when custom chips ramp hard in the second half. They are not evidence for the optical half in particular. What showed me the slip was reading the 10-Q's note against my own sentence. The claim does not rest on the prepayments, and the revenue mix still supports it. The piece and the claim stay exactly as published.

## What the market prices in, and where I differ

At US$284.68 Marvell is worth about US$250 billion on its 876.9 million common shares. Counting Nvidia's preferred stock as converted, it is about US$256 billion. It has about US$1.0 billion more debt than cash. The shares rose 5.8 per cent on the day of the Investor Day, to US$287.01. They trade at 100 times fiscal 2026 non-GAAP earnings of US$2.84, and 38 times my fiscal 2028 estimate.

**What the price needs.** By October 2027 the market will be pricing fiscal 2029. At 33 times, the median forward multiple of six peers, US$284.68 needs fiscal 2029 earnings of US$8.63 a share. At my 43 per cent operating margin that needs revenue of about US$22.5 billion. That is management's US$20 billion for fiscal 2028, already up 67 per cent, plus only another 12 per cent. Marvell's custom target alone adds about US$8 billion in fiscal 2029, on my fiscal 2028 custom estimate. So the price allows for most of it to slip.

**Where I differ.** I do not take management's path. Getting from US$20 billion to the US$80 billion midpoint in three years means growing about 59 per cent a year, and I take 40 per cent for fiscal 2029. Even so, the price needs less than I assume.

The growth beyond fiscal 2028 leans towards custom, and the Google deal points that way. Google's warrant vests one tranche for each US$500 million of custom revenue, across 240 tranches, so it covers up to US$120 billion through fiscal 2033. Custom work is lumpier, and the buyers are stronger. Non-GAAP gross margin has slipped from 62.4 per cent in the quarter to May 2024 to 58.9 per cent, and Marvell guides 57.5 to 58.5 per cent this quarter because of the custom ramp. Its fiscal 2031 model allows 56 to 59 per cent, "influenced by product mix".

So I pay no more than the peer median multiple for that growth. The upside comes from earnings, not from a rerating. And the toll stays the largest line: interconnect is 47 per cent of the fiscal 2031 midpoint.

## Valuation, with the working

I value Marvell on non-GAAP earnings per share, the basis on which it guides, twelve months out. By October 2027 the market will price fiscal 2029, so that is the year I value. Non-GAAP figures leave out stock-based pay, which was US$326 million in the August quarter, 11.9 per cent of revenue. Under GAAP, Marvell earned US$0.38 a share in the first half against US$1.75 non-GAAP.

**Fiscal 2027.** The first two quarters are reported. For the third I use the guide's midpoints: US$3.15 billion of revenue, 58 per cent gross margin and US$655 million of costs, giving US$1.10 a share. The fourth quarter is what "roughly $12 billion" for the year leaves: US$3.69 billion, at 38.8 per cent operating margin.

**Fiscal 2028.** I take management's new US$20 billion and a 58 per cent gross margin, in the same range as this half. Costs grow at about half the rate of revenue, as the Investor Day model repeats, and tax is the guided 13 per cent. That gives 41.0 per cent operating margin and US$7.54 a share, against a consensus of US$6.75 compiled before the Investor Day.

**Fiscal 2029.** Growth of 40 per cent to US$28 billion, at 43 per cent operating margin, on the way to Marvell's fiscal 2031 target of 44 to 46 per cent. Tax is the long-term 15 per cent, on 940 million shares. That gives US$10.78 a share.

## Risks, and what would change my view

**The cycle turns.** If the largest cloud companies slow their spending, Marvell's volume falls whatever happens to its share of each link. The bear case is this, and it is a 46 per cent fall.

**Custom is lumpy.** Custom programmes are won and lost by generation, and the buyers run their own chip teams. Gross margin has already slipped as custom grows. The Google warrant aligns one large buyer, but it also costs shares: if fully vested it would add about 16 million net shares at today's price.

**Targets are not results.** The fiscal 2031 range is a plan set out in a presentation, not guidance. Marvell had not filed the Investor Day material with the SEC by 8 October.

**The translation moves.** The claim's own falsifier. If 1.6T or 3.2T links drop the DSP for linear optics, or co-packaged optics spread from scale-up into scale-out, the toll moves to whoever makes the switch chip. Marvell makes switch chips and now co-packaged parts, so it would compete on different ground rather than lose outright.

**Few, large buyers.** Marvell's ten largest customers took 82 per cent of fiscal 2026 revenue, up from 81 per cent a year earlier. One distributor took 44 per cent of revenue in the August quarter, against 34 per cent a year earlier.

**Quality of earnings and dilution.** Stock-based pay more than doubled on a year earlier in the August quarter. The Celestial AI deal can still add up to 22.4 million shares and about US$233 million in cash through fiscal 2029, and Nvidia's preferred stock converts into 21.8 million shares.

**What would change my view.** Out of the long and into no call: a price above about US$310 on unchanged estimates, or evidence that fiscal 2029 earnings will fall below about US$9.92, such as Marvell cutting its custom target back towards US$10 billion. Into more conviction: the Investor Day targets repeated in an SEC filing or a results release, and data centre revenue in the October quarter rising more than 20 per cent on the quarter before, as guided.

The third quarter results, for the quarter to October, are expected in early December. Marvell has not yet announced the date; last year's fell on 2 December.

## Conclusion

My piece of 28 September argued that Marvell is paid every time AI chips talk, through the signal processor in each optical plug. The year of results behind it supports that claim, and the Investor Day kept interconnect the largest line in Marvell's plan to fiscal 2031. I was wrong about one thing: Marvell never said its US$1 billion of prepayments was for optical DSPs.

At US$284.68 the price needs only about US$22.5 billion of fiscal 2029 revenue. I take US$28 billion, below management's path, at the peer median multiple. My call is long, with medium conviction and a target of US$355, within a range of US$153 to US$517. Medium, because the plan is still a presentation, not yet results.
