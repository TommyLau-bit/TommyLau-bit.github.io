Mitsubishi Heavy Industries' shares closed at ¥3,363 on 2 November 2026. That is 35 per cent below their record close of ¥5,195 on 2 March, and the lowest close since July 2025.

My piece of 22 September argued that a data centre which builds its own power does not escape the queue. It swaps the grid's queue for the turbine makers'. The piece rests mostly on GE Vernova. Mitsubishi Heavy, known as MHI, is one of the other two makers of the largest gas turbines, and the piece cites its finance chief on delivery dates and prices.

MHI's own figures support the claim. The question for an investor is different, because MHI is not a gas turbine company. It also builds nuclear plant equipment, aircraft parts, and missiles, warships and military aircraft for Japan's Ministry of Defense. So this note asks two things. How much of MHI's value is gas turbines for the AI power build, and how much is defence? And what does ¥3,363 assume about each?

## The thesis

Think of the well from the piece. The council's water main is years away, so everyone on the street decides to sink a well, and the big drilling firm is booked for years. A buyer of that firm gets two things. Its revenue for the next few years is close to certain, because the diary is full. And each new booking is priced higher, because the diary is full.

MHI's gas turbine business is that drilling firm. It sells gas turbine combined cycle plants, called GTCC, which pair a gas turbine with a steam turbine that runs on its waste heat. In fiscal 2025, the year to March 2026, GTCC took ¥2,653 billion of orders against ¥992 billion of revenue, 2.7 times. Its order book passed ¥5 trillion in March, about five years of that revenue. In the June quarter orders were ¥930 billion against ¥275 billion of revenue, 3.4 times.

The units say the same. MHI had 48 large turbines, those above 100 megawatts, on order in March 2025. By June 2026 it had 80, about 35 gigawatts. It booked 35 in fiscal 2025 and 10 more in the June quarter, four for the United States.

The claim is about the length of that queue, and MHI's finance chief, Hiroshi Nishio, gave the length himself. According to MHI's summary of the results briefing dated 6 August, projects now under discussion "will generally be delivered around 2030 to 2031". Orders booked in the June quarter are due mostly between 2028 and 2030. "Order pricing has not declined", he said, and the margin on new orders keeps improving.

The buyers also pay to stand in line, as the piece found at GE Vernova. MHI raised its free cash flow forecast for this year from ¥300 billion to ¥600 billion, mainly because "advances received in GTCC have increased more than expected". Contract liabilities, the balance sheet line that holds those prepayments, rose from ¥1,435 billion in March 2025 to ¥2,483 billion in June 2026. In May Nishio said customers keep paying in advance "to secure production slots".

MHI is not building its way out of the queue either. The chief executive, Eisaku Ito, told investors on 27 May that MHI aims to ship 30 per cent or more units in fiscal 2028 than its current capacity allows, and then about 50 per cent more again, around twice the fiscal 2024 level. He gave no year for the second step. The first comes mostly from more than 1,000 changes on the factory floor, not new buildings. In August Nishio said MHI is building the order book "while carefully monitoring progress on the capacity expansion", rather than booking twice as many orders at once.

So the claim holds at a second maker, on both of its tests. Lead times are not falling, and the new output is being added without clearing the backlog. I checked the piece's MHI sentences against MHI's own Q&amp;A summaries and presentations, and they stand.

MHI does add one caution the piece did not have. On 27 May Ito said world orders for gas turbines used in power generation reached 96 gigawatts in 2025, but that MHI expects about 70 gigawatts a year on average over the next five years. On 12 May he had said that is about 10 gigawatts above MHI's earlier view of 50 to 60. So MHI expects the queue to stay long because supply is tight, not because world orders keep rising, though Ito still expects MHI's own unit orders to grow over the next several years. GE Vernova said in July that it sees supply and demand for heavy-duty turbines very balanced for six years, so the two makers do not disagree. But MHI is the one saying aloud that 2025 may have been the top for world orders.

The money shows up in margin. Energy Systems, the segment that holds GTCC, earned a business profit margin of 13.0 per cent in fiscal 2025. Business profit is MHI's measure of operating profit, which includes gains on asset sales. MHI forecasts 15.5 per cent for fiscal 2026, and the June quarter reached 18.9 per cent, including about ¥25 billion of one-off items. Nishio said even without those items the margin beat the full-year assumption. MHI does not report GTCC's profit separately. An analyst on the August call put it near 20 per cent, and Nishio replied that the margin should keep improving as higher-margin orders reach revenue. I assume 20 per cent in fiscal 2026.

## What the market prices in, and where I differ

At ¥3,363 MHI is worth about ¥11.3 trillion on 3,361 million shares. The shares trade at 29.7 times MHI's own fiscal 2026 forecast of ¥113.09 a share, and 25.0 times the consensus of ¥134.68 compiled by stockanalysis.com on 2 November. The average target of 17 analysts there is ¥5,469.

**How much of MHI is gas turbines, and how much is defence.** On MHI's fiscal 2026 forecast, GTCC is ¥1,100 billion of ¥5,400 billion of revenue, 20 per cent. Defense and Space is ¥1,250 billion, 23 per cent. On profit the order changes. If GTCC earns 20 per cent, it makes ¥220 billion, 41 per cent of the group's ¥540 billion forecast. Aircraft, Defense and Space, which also holds commercial aircraft parts, makes ¥170 billion, 31 per cent. In my sum of the parts, GTCC is 46 per cent of the equity value and Aircraft, Defense and Space 29 per cent. So MHI is a gas turbine company first and a defence company second, with nuclear third.

Defence is large and steady rather than fast. Defense and Space took about ¥1.9 trillion of orders in fiscal 2024 and ¥1.7 trillion in fiscal 2025, and MHI expects ¥1.5 trillion this year. Revenue rose from ¥828 billion to ¥1,145 billion and is forecast at ¥1,250 billion. Aircraft, Defense and Space as a whole had ¥3.9 trillion on order in June. Nishio said revenue should rise "gradually" towards the level of annual orders as MHI invests in production capacity.

**What the price implies.** I value each part on my fiscal 2027 business profit, the year to March 2028. With everything except GTCC at my base multiples, the price leaves about ¥4.5 trillion for GTCC. That is 15.9 times my fiscal 2027 estimate of its profit, below the 20 times I use. Put the other way, with GTCC at 20 times, the price leaves Aircraft, Defense and Space at 12.1 times, against a median of 19.8 times trailing EV to EBIT for Rheinmetall, BAE Systems, Thales and Leonardo. The market is not paying full peer multiples for both businesses at once.

That sounds like a long, and the analysts' targets say so. I differ on three points.

**The cash is mostly the customers'.** MHI reports net cash of ¥1,164 billion. But contract liabilities rose ¥1,049 billion in fifteen months, and that money will be spent building the turbines it paid for. Nishio said as much in August: the cash "does not mean that it can immediately be treated as surplus funds". My profit estimates already count the margin on those orders, so I deduct the cost still to come on the rise in prepayments, 80 per cent of it, or ¥839 billion. Counting all the net cash as surplus would add about ¥250 a share and put the base value 18 per cent above the price. This one choice decides whether the base clears my 15 per cent bar for a long.

**The queue caps the volume.** Revenue on these contracts is recognised as the work progresses, and the factory sets the pace. MHI's 30 per cent step lands in fiscal 2028. I take GTCC revenue up 18 per cent in fiscal 2027, at a 22 per cent margin, as higher-priced orders reach revenue.

**MHI is choosing price over share.** On 12 May Ito said that in fiscal 2025 the makers that used the most capacity won the most share, and that MHI hopes to win back the top share as demand normalises. Its 16 gigawatts of orders that year were roughly a sixth of the 96 gigawatts ordered in calendar 2025, on my rough comparison of different years. That is good for margin and for the queue, but it means MHI is not the maker adding volume fastest.

The fall since March has no single company cause that I could find. The shares fell 1.9 per cent on the full-year results of 12 May and rose 7.7 per cent on the first quarter results of 4 August. They have fallen 12 per cent since the end of 2025.

## Valuation, with the working

I value MHI as a sum of its parts: each business at a multiple of my fiscal 2027 business profit, which the market will price as the year ahead by October 2027. I split Energy Systems into GTCC and the rest, which is nuclear, steam power, aero engines and compressors. I add cash and investments, deduct debt, minority interests, the pension liability and the cost still to come on the prepayments. Then I divide by 3,361 million shares.

## Risks, and what would change my view

**Orders past the peak.** MHI itself expects world orders to fall from 96 gigawatts in 2025 to about 70 a year over the next five years, and until May it expected 50 to 60. If orders fall faster than capacity rises, the queue shortens and the multiple falls with it. That is most of the bear case.

**Execution and one-offs.** Fiscal 2025 carried about ¥30 billion of losses on steam power projects, ¥20 billion of it in South Africa, and a ¥30 billion goodwill impairment. The June quarter's margin was lifted by about ¥25 billion of one-off items. Large fixed-price plants can swing a margin of 15 per cent quickly.

**Capacity.** The doubling has no date. If the 2027 medium-term plan pushes it beyond 2030, the queue stays long but GTCC revenue grows more slowly than my base case.

**Defence.** MHI's defence orders depend on Japan's budget. A change in budget plans, or delays in new defence production capacity, would slow the step up in revenue. Overseas work, such as the Australian frigate programme booked in fiscal 2025, carries terms MHI is less used to, as Ito said in May.

**The yen.** MHI assumes ¥150 to the US dollar this year, and expects about US$3.2 billion of dollar amounts to affect business profit. The yen averaged ¥157.2 in the June quarter. A stronger yen would cut the yen value of the queue without changing the queue itself.

**The cash.** If MHI treats the prepayments as surplus and returns them, my bridge is too cautious. Nishio said the 2027 plan will set out the approach to capital allocation.

**What would change my view.** Into a long: a price below about ¥3,200, where my base value would sit 15 per cent above it, or a 2027 plan that dates the doubling of gas turbine output to fiscal 2030 or earlier. Towards a short: a price above about ¥4,950, where my base value would sit 25 per cent below it.

**Catalysts.** First half results are scheduled for 6 November 2026 at 13:30 Tokyo time. I will watch large frame unit orders against fiscal 2025's 35, the Energy Systems margin against 15.5 per cent, and contract liabilities. Third quarter results are due in early February 2027 and full-year results with the 2027 medium-term plan around May 2027; MHI has not announced either date.

## Conclusion

The September piece argued that building your own power swaps the grid's queue for the turbine makers'. MHI's figures confirm it at a second maker. Its gas turbine order book holds about five years of revenue, new talks are for 2030 to 2031, prices are not falling, and buyers pay in advance.

But the queue that makes GTCC's revenue so visible also paces it, and a large part of MHI's cash belongs to the buyers in it. At ¥3,363 the market values gas turbines at about 16 times my fiscal 2027 estimate, or defence at about 12 times, a discount but not a deep one. My call is no call, with medium conviction. My base value is ¥3,713, 10 per cent above the price, inside a range of ¥2,617 to ¥4,693. The view is wrong if the shares close above ¥4,200 or below ¥2,500 in October 2027, if MHI books fewer than 25 large turbines in fiscal 2026, or if the Energy Systems margin lands below 14 per cent.
