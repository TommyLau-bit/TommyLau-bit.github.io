Seagate's shares closed at US$644.58 on 30 October 2026. That is 41 per cent below their June high, in a quarter when Seagate earned the highest margin in its history.

The worry is easy to state. Hard drives are a cyclical business, Seagate's margin has never been near this level, and on 2 October Toshiba said its Philippine drive plant will nearly double its capacity. The fear is that a supplier riding a shortage is about to find out where the shortage ends.

My piece on the chip supply chain put Seagate on its watch list. It is one of very few qualified makers, it is booked years ahead, but it grows without a new building. Seagate's latest results support all three. They also add something the piece did not have: the price per terabyte is now rising.

So the question is not whether the drives are sold. It is what the price already assumes about the price per terabyte once the contracts roll over.

## The thesis

Think of a removals firm with a fixed fleet. As bookings grow, it does not buy more vans; it packs each one tighter, and customers book their move a year or more ahead. For two years that meant more boxes at the same price per box. Now the firm is charging more per box as well. The question for an investor is whether that lasts once a rival buys more vans.

**More terabytes, not more drives.** Seagate shipped 198 exabytes of nearline drives, the high-capacity kind data centres use, in the quarter to 2 October. That is up about 24 per cent on a year, and fiscal 2026 brought 695 exabytes against 497 the year before. In the December 2025 quarter it shipped 26 per cent more exabytes than a year earlier "on similar number of units". On 27 October it said it meets exabyte growth "by increasing capacity per drive rather than increasing drive unit production". On the call it put capital spending at 4 to 6 per cent of revenue, enough for exabyte supply growth in the mid 20s per cent. Its HAMR drives, which heat each bit with a laser so it can be smaller, carry that density.

**The horizon lengthened.** Seagate said its nearline supply was allocated through 2026 in January, and largely through 2027 in April. On 27 October it said the "vast majority" of data centre nearline supply is allocated through 2028, with some cloud agreements running to 2030. I checked the piece's Seagate figures against the supplementals, the release and the 10-K; they stand.

**Now the price moves too.** Seagate does not publish a price per terabyte, so I derive one: Data Center revenue over nearline exabytes, both from its supplementals. From late 2024 to early 2026 it held between US$13.30 and US$14.29. In the last two quarters it rose to US$15.04 and then US$17.42, up 31 per cent on a year. Seagate said on the call that pricing rose faster than before, mainly by repricing contracts, and that it now passes higher component costs through. That is what took the non-GAAP gross margin from 40.1 per cent to 57.8 per cent in a year. In the quarter to 2 October revenue was US$4.23 billion, up 61 per cent, and earnings per share were US$7.68. Seagate guides to US$4.8 billion and US$9.20 a share for the December quarter.

**The piece left one question unanswered.** Allocated is not priced. Seagate's build-to-order contracts set configuration and price for nearline shipments through calendar 2027 only. It expects to start finalising 2028 prices over the next few quarters. The horizon in my piece's chart is a horizon of volume. The price on its last year is still open, and that is the year the share price now depends on.

The piece's claim itself rests on ASML and Micron, not on Seagate, so nothing here tests it. The note tests the watch item, and finds it holding and strengthening.

## What the market prices in, and where I differ

At US$644.58 Seagate is worth about US$148 billion on its 230 million shares, with net debt of US$1.1 billion. The shares trade at 16.1 times fiscal 2027 consensus of US$39.98 and 9.9 times fiscal 2028 consensus of US$65.34, both from stockanalysis.com on 2 November. On the December quarter guide, annualised, they trade at 17.5 times.

**Toshiba moved the price.** The shares closed at a high of US$1,094.04 on 22 June and fell 30 per cent in October alone. On 2 October Toshiba said its Philippine drive plant had begun making nearline drives on an expanded line. The plant aims to nearly double its annual capacity, measured in storage, by Toshiba's fiscal 2027 against fiscal 2025. Seagate's shares fell 10.2 per cent that day. Western Digital, the other listed maker, guides to a 60.5 per cent non-GAAP gross margin for its December quarter, so the whole industry is earning at this level.

**Hard drives are already priced as steadier than flash.** Seagate and Western Digital trade at 16.1 and 14.8 times 2027 consensus. The flash makers Sandisk and Micron trade at 5.1 and 5.0 times. The market already believes three makers, long contracts and no new factories make this cycle milder than memory's. I agree it is milder. The question is how much milder.

**What the price needs.** I value the cycle, not the year. The value at October 2027 is net cash then, plus the earnings above normal still to come, plus 14 times normal earnings. On my base fiscal 2028, US$644.58 needs normal earnings of US$42.74 a share. That is 2.7 times fiscal 2026 and 80 per cent of my fiscal 2028. On my normal revenue it is a 38.2 per cent net margin, against 29.0 per cent in fiscal 2026 and a ten-year GAAP average of 9.5 per cent. Put another way, at my 30 per cent margin it needs a normal price of US$18.09 per terabyte at the scale of 2030, above the record US$17.42 just reached.

**Where I differ.** My normal year lets nearline exabytes grow 25 per cent a year for four years, to 1,697 exabytes, at the US$13.91 per terabyte Seagate averaged over fiscal 2025 and 2026. That average was not a glut price: by January 2026, with the price still near it, Seagate had already allocated its output a year ahead. I give it a 30 per cent net margin, slightly above fiscal 2026, because each HAMR generation cuts the cost of a terabyte. The market assumes the 2026 price rise survives Toshiba's added Philippine capacity and the next round of contracts. It may, because no maker is building a drive factory. That is why I do not go short.

## Valuation, with the working

All forecasts below are mine. Income figures are Seagate's non-GAAP measures, in US$ billion except per share.

**Fiscal 2027.** The first quarter is reported and the second is the guide. After that revenue is US$5.15 billion and US$5.45 billion, at operating margins of 57 and 58 per cent, with tax at the guided 16 per cent on 233 million shares. That gives revenue of US$19.63 billion and earnings per share of US$38.79, 3.0 per cent below consensus.

**Fiscal 2028.** Nearline exabytes grow 25 per cent and the price per terabyte 5 per cent, at a 58 per cent operating margin. Earnings per share are US$53.74, 17.8 per cent below consensus, because 2028 prices are not yet set and Toshiba's capacity arrives during the year.

**The base value.** Net cash at October 2027 is worth US$31.64 a share. The earnings above normal in fiscal 2028, and half of that excess again in fiscal 2029, discounted at 10 per cent, add US$26.62. Fourteen times normal earnings of US$33.61 adds US$470.52. Together they make US$529.

**Peers.** On consensus for the year ending in 2027, Western Digital trades at 14.8 times, Sandisk at 5.1 and Micron at 5.0, against Seagate's 16.1. Toshiba has been privately held since its delisting in December 2023 and has no listed multiple.

Fiscal 2027 and the cash at October 2027 are the same in all three cases. That is deliberate: 2027 prices are set in contracts, so a price turn shows from fiscal 2028. Weighted 25, 50 and 25 per cent, the cases give about US$549, 15 per cent below the price. The bear case is not fanciful: Seagate's revenue fell 37 per cent in fiscal 2023, and the shares are already 41 per cent off their June high.

**The band.** I go long when my base value is at least 15 per cent above the price, and short when it is at least 25 per cent below. At US$529, 18 per cent below, Seagate sits in between. So my call is no call.

## Risks, and what would change my view

**Durability, on the upside.** Seagate says it is talking to customers well past 2028, and some cloud agreements already run to 2030. If 2028 prices are set above 2027's and the 2029 book fills the same way, the normal year moves towards my bull case, a 38 per cent rise. Flash, the obvious substitute, is short and dear too: Micron said on 30 September that its NAND prices rose by about 30 per cent in a single quarter.

**Supply, on the downside.** Toshiba is adding capacity now, and every maker sees the same price signal. Seagate's revenue fell 37 per cent in fiscal 2023, the last downturn. A price cut in the 2028 contracts would show first in revenue per terabyte, and my bear case is a 61 per cent fall.

**Execution and costs.** HAMR needs longer manufacturing cycle times and more tooling, and Seagate said scrap and yield losses during transitions weigh on costs. External component costs are much higher than a year ago. Inventory rose to US$1.70 billion from US$1.57 billion in the quarter as Seagate prepared manufacturing for Mozaic 4 customer qualifications.

**Double ordering.** Buyers in a boom book early and sometimes twice. Allocation shows commitment, not use, and the filings cannot separate the two.

**What would change my view.** A price at or below about US$460 would point long and one at or above about US$705 would point short, with my numbers unchanged. The view is wrong if, by October 2027, Seagate's supplementals show Data Center revenue per nearline exabyte below the same quarter a year earlier, or Seagate says 2028 prices are lower than 2027's. That points to my bear case. It is also wrong if Seagate says the vast majority of its 2029 supply is allocated and 2028 prices are set above 2027's, which lifts normal earnings towards my bull case.

The second quarter results are expected in late January 2027. Seagate has not yet announced the date.

## Conclusion

My piece put Seagate on its watch list: few makers, booked years ahead, but growing without a new building. Its results support that, and add a fact the piece did not have: the price per terabyte is up 31 per cent in a year. The shares are a different question. At 9.9 times consensus fiscal 2028 earnings they look cheap, but the price needs that rise to become the normal at the scale of 2030.

My base value is US$529, 18 per cent below the price, within a range of US$249 to US$889. My call is NO CALL, leaning short, with low conviction. Low, because the base sits seven points from the short line and rests on my normal price per terabyte, while 2027 prices are set and still rising and 2028 prices will not be known for months.
