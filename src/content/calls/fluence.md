Fluence's shares closed at US$7.43 on 9 October 2026. That is 62 per cent below the end of 2025 and 77 per cent below the highest close, US$32.23 on 3 February.

My piece of 12 October argued that Fluence is not short of batteries or buyers. It is short of battery systems that count as American-made, because American tax rules reward them, and the new Houston line that makes them fell badly behind this summer.

The filings support that claim. The harder question for an investor is what it is worth, and the honest answer today is that nobody knows yet whether Houston is a fault of months or of years. At US$7.43 the market prices something in between, and so do I.

## The thesis

Think of the boiler shop from the piece. Its customers get a large discount from the council only if the boiler was built in this country. Its warehouse is full of cheap imported boilers, and the one local workshop is welding by hand while its robot is fixed. The shop has more orders than ever. What it can deliver this winter is set by that one workshop.

Fluence is that shop, at the scale of power stations. It does not make battery cells. It designs the system, has contract factories assemble it and writes the software that runs it. At the end of June 2026 its backlog, the value of signed work not yet turned into revenue, was a record US$6.4 billion. It expects 48 to 53 per cent of that to become revenue in the next twelve months.

Demand is not the problem. Orders in the June quarter were more than US$1.44 billion, nearly triple the US$508.8 million of a year earlier. In the nine months to June it contracted 4.2 gigawatts of storage, against 1.9 a year before, and its pipeline of likely projects grew 34 per cent to 163.7 gigawatt hours. By July it had about US$850 million of data centre business, including a first large order behind the meter and about US$550 million of awards from a hyperscaler that, on the August call, were not yet purchase orders.

Delivery is the problem. In November 2025 Fluence guided revenue for the year to September 2026 of US$3.2 to 3.6 billion. On 5 August it cut that to US$2.9 to 3.1 billion. On 16 September it cut again, to about US$2.4 billion, with an adjusted EBITDA loss of about US$200 million against a guide of US$40 to 60 million of profit ten months earlier. Adjusted EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, with share pay and one-off items stripped out.

The September release names the cause plainly. Demand "has remained strong", the international supply chain "has continued to work well", and delays at "our contract manufacturing facility in Houston" are "the primary reason". On the August call the chief executive described Houston as a fully automated plant for enclosures, the steel boxes that hold the battery modules, sized for 15 gigawatt hours a year and running on generators while it waited for its grid connection. Trade press reports of the September call say it averaged under one unit a day in August against a plan of eleven, reached three a day in early September, and that more than 80 per cent of the cut came from American production. I could not verify those figures against the replay.

The money shows where the strain sits. Gross margin in the June quarter was 5.1 per cent, against 14.8 per cent a year earlier. The quarterly report names liquidated damages, the late delivery penalties agreed in contracts, as the first reason for the June quarter, ahead of cost overruns on newer products and higher battery prices. For the nine months to June it also names cost increases on certain projects produced in the United States. Customers in the Americas made up 56 per cent of revenue in the nine months to June.

**Where I was wrong in October.** The piece said: "Its factories overseas, the company said, kept working well." That is what Fluence said on 16 September. But it followed the line that Fluence "cut its revenue forecast twice in six weeks", and the first of those cuts, on 5 August, blamed two things: "production issues at a new international contract manufacturing facility" and construction delays at the new American one. On the August call management said a new China facility's first output had needed rework and was then at full production. What showed me the slip was the August release itself. It does not change the view: the second, larger cut was mainly American, and the claim stands.

So the claim holds on the evidence so far. What it does not tell an investor is how long Houston takes. Fluence says it will publish American production figures, actual and forecast, and a fiscal 2027 business plan with its fiscal 2026 results. Those numbers are the test of the claim and of the shares.

## What the market prices in, and where I differ

At US$7.43 Fluence is worth about US$1.37 billion on its 184.6 million shares, counting the 41.4 million Class B-1 shares AES can redeem for Class A shares one for one. At the end of June it held US$365 million of cash, including restricted cash, against US$400 million of convertible notes due 2030, which convert at about US$21.35, so net debt was about US$35 million.

The September quarter's cash use is not yet reported. Free cash flow was minus US$299 million in the nine months to June, and the S&P Global consensus for the full year implies about minus US$133 million in the final quarter. I assume US$130 million, which puts net debt at about US$165 million and the enterprise value at about US$1.54 billion. These are my estimates, not Fluence's.

That is 0.64 times this year's revenue guide and 0.47 times the S&P Global consensus of US$3.25 billion for the year to September 2027. The average target of 23 analysts is US$10, but the median is US$8.50 and the range runs from US$3 to US$23. Fourteen rate it a hold. About 15 per cent of the shares are sold short. In plain terms, the market expects Fluence to recover, but not fully and not soon.

I differ on three points.

**Next year is not as safe as consensus.** On the August call Fluence said US$2.2 billion of its backlog was scheduled for fiscal 2027, before the September cut pushed more work into that year. Consensus of US$3.25 billion needs the Houston fix early and new orders delivered within the year. Fluence's own September release lists plans to right-size its revenue growth, and aims for neutral to positive operating cash flow without new capital. I take US$3.0 billion, a little below consensus.

**The margin comes back, but slowly.** Penalties and launch costs should fade once deliveries run on time, and Fluence earned a 13.7 per cent adjusted gross margin as recently as fiscal 2025. I assume 10.5 per cent next year and 13 per cent the year after. Even then Fluence earns little: on my numbers adjusted EBITDA is about US$20 million in fiscal 2027 and US$148 million, a 4.1 per cent margin, in fiscal 2028.

**The balance sheet is the bear case, not the orders.** Total liquidity was US$863 million at the end of June, including the revolving credit facility. That facility carries a US$150 million minimum liquidity test through 31 December 2026, and a leverage ratio test that starts on 1 January 2027, while adjusted EBITDA is negative. A further slip at Houston would test both, and the shelf registration filed in May lets Fluence issue new shares if it must.

The share price has swung hard on each result. It fell 34.6 per cent the day after the first quarter results in February, rose 39.9 per cent after the second quarter in May, and fell 15.4 per cent after the September cut. The next set of figures will move it as much.

## Valuation, with the working

I value Fluence on enterprise value to revenue in fiscal 2028, the year to September 2028, which is what the market will price by October 2027. Its EBITDA is too thin to carry the value alone: on my numbers, one point of gross margin moves fiscal 2028 EBITDA by about a quarter. I cross-check every case against EBITDA.

**The multiple.** I use 0.45 times fiscal 2028 revenue for the base case. At my 4.1 per cent margin that is about 11 times EBITDA, below the median of 13 times trailing EBITDA for Sungrow, Nextracker, Shoals and Array Technologies (stockanalysis.com, 12 October). ## Risks, and what would change my view

**Houston.** Everything turns on it. On the August call Fluence expected full production in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, the three months to December 2026. Trade press reports of the September call say the line was then at three units a day against eleven, after switching to manual welding. A new chief operating officer, Bernerd Da Santos, formerly at AES, started on 15 September to run supply chain and manufacturing.

**Cash and covenants.** The leverage ratio test under the revolving credit facility starts on 1 January 2027. A weak first quarter of fiscal 2027 could force a waiver, a smaller facility or new shares. Fluence says its aim is to fund the backlog without external capital.

**Penalties.** Liquidated damages reduce revenue as projects slip. Trade press reports of the September call put late delivery penalties for the year in the tens of millions of dollars, with figures that differ between reports. Each month Houston runs late adds to them.

**Data centre awards.** The US$550 million of hyperscaler awards were not purchase orders in August. Data centre buyers want speed, and delays are exactly what could send them elsewhere. Tesla Energy and Sungrow both build complete systems.

**Policy.** The domestic content bonus and the prohibited foreign entity rules are what make American-made systems scarce. Fluence believes its American suppliers comply with the rules under Treasury guidance of 12 February 2026. A change in that guidance would cut both ways: less scarcity, but less pressure on Houston.

**Holders and lawsuits.** Siemens and AES each hold about 41.4 million shares, and selling holders can still sell 94.7 million shares under the May 2026 shelf. A securities class action, Hatweek v. Fluence Energy, No. 1:26-cv-08475 (S.D.N.Y.), was filed on 28 September 2026 for buyers between 24 November 2025 and 16 September 2026. The lead plaintiff deadline is 27 November 2026.

**What would change my view.** A price below about US$6.85 would put my base value 15 per cent above it and turn my view long on unchanged estimates. So would Fluence's first American production figures showing Houston at or near 11 units a day, with a fiscal 2027 plan of at least US$3.0 billion. A price above about US$10.51 would put my base value 25 per cent below it and raise the case for a short.

The fiscal 2026 results, the first American production figures and the fiscal 2027 plan are due together. Fluence has not announced the date. Last year's full-year results came on 24 November 2025, with the date announced on 29 October.

## Conclusion

The October piece argued that Fluence's American deliveries are limited by how fast it can make systems that count as American-made, not by demand. Its filings say so: a record US$6.4 billion backlog, orders nearly tripled, and a September release that blames Houston and says demand stays strong. One correction: the first cut, in August, also named a new overseas factory, since fixed. The claim stands.

But a claim about a bottleneck does not say how long it lasts. At US$7.43 the shares price a recovery that is partial and late. My base case is worth about US$7.88, 6 per cent above the price, inside a range of US$3.04 to US$15.08. My call is no call, with medium conviction. I would revisit below about US$6.85, or when Fluence's first American production figures show Houston near 11 units a day.
