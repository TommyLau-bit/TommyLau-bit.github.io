Bloom Energy's shares closed at US$286.65 on 5 October 2026. They have more than tripled since the end of 2025, on a run of orders that proves what my September piece argued: buyers pay Bloom for speed.

Oracle plans to procure up to 2.8 GW of Bloom's fuel cells. AEP, one of America's largest utilities, exercised most of a 900 MW option in January, an order of about US$2.65 billion. Brookfield, which finances many of the projects, lifted its framework from US$5 billion to US$25 billion in June. Revenue in the second quarter was US$1.07 billion, up 166 per cent.

So the claim is holding. What I got wrong was how Bloom gets paid for it. Splitting its margin by line shows that the premium for speed arrives as volume, not as price. Volume is set by a factory, and the share price already assumes that factory grows a lot.

## The thesis

Go back to the mobile hotspot from the September piece. You pay more per gigabyte because the cable engineer is months away. Now picture the shop that sells the hotspots. Its queue is round the block, yet its prices are much the same as last year. It sells more boxes, not dearer ones, and it can only sell as many as its one van can deliver.

Bloom is that shop. Its solid oxide fuel cells arrive on a lorry, sit beside a building and turn natural gas into electricity within months. A grid connection for a large new load can take years, and the makers of gas turbines are largely sold out. Bloom's 10-K counts 2,300 GW of projects waiting in interconnection queues at the end of 2024.

The buyers are proof that time is the product. Revenue in the first half of 2026 was 3.2 times the first half of 2024. On 13 April Oracle signed to procure up to 2.8 GW, with 1.2 GW already contracted. On 28 July Bloom raised its 2026 revenue guide to US$3.9 to 4.2 billion, from US$3.1 to 3.3 billion in February. All the big American cloud companies have approved its systems, according to the company.

Where the money shows up matters for the shares. If Bloom were charging for scarcity, its price per box would rise and so would the margin on each box. The place to look is product gross margin, the share of each product sale left after the cost of making it. It was 36.8 per cent in 2024, 35.2 per cent in 2025 and 35.6 per cent in the first half of 2026. Revenue tripled, and the margin on the product barely moved.

**Where I was wrong in September.** In the piece I wrote that gross margin rising from 26.7 to 33.4 per cent while volume more than doubled was "the time premium, expressed in money". That was wrong. The total margin rose mainly because of mix. Products, which earn far more than installation and service, went from 74 to 88 per cent of revenue between the two quarters. Product margin also recovered, from 33.0 to 36.5 per cent, after a weak 2025, and service added a little. But over a full year, the product margin, where a premium for speed would show, has not moved: 36.8 per cent in 2024, 35.2 per cent in 2025 and 35.6 per cent in the first half of 2026.

What changed my view was splitting the margin by line, using the revenue and cost lines in Bloom's income statement. I had not done that for the piece. Two smaller points follow from the filings. I wrote that the service backlog of about US$14 billion was contracts for up to twenty years, the part least exposed to the grid catching up. The 10-K says the contracts run 5 to 20 years, but customers can end them for convenience each year. And I wrote that Oracle had committed to up to 2.8 GW. It intends to procure up to 2.8 GW, of which 1.2 GW is contracted.

The claim itself survives: customers are paying for an earlier switch-on, and they are paying in orders. But the margin test the claim names is weaker than I thought, because margin never tracked scarcity in the first place. The better tests are volume, the time from order to power, and whether Oracle's further 1.6 GW is contracted. The piece and the claim stay exactly as published, so the record shows the mistake.

## What the market prices in, and where I differ

At US$286.65 Bloom is worth about US$84 billion on 294.5 million shares, with about US$0.14 billion more cash than debt. That is 106 times the midpoint of its own 2026 earnings guide of US$2.55 to 2.85 a share. On my estimates and fully diluted shares it is 103 times 2026, 65 times 2027 and 48 times 2028.

The market has paid for each step up in volume. The shares rose from US$176.67 on 13 April to US$219.03 on 14 April, as the Oracle expansion was announced, and from US$166.84 to US$207.12 in the two days after the second quarter results. They fell from US$269.57 to US$254.29 on 8 July, when a short seller, Hunterbrook, alleged that Bloom relies on Chinese scandium for its cells. Bloom rejected the report and says its supply covers demand and backlog.

**What the price needs.** By October 2027 the market will be pricing 2028. At 40 times earnings, US$286.65 needs 2028 earnings of US$7.17 a share, close to the US$7.00 consensus of the two analysts who publish one. At my base margins and costs that needs revenue of about US$10.0 billion.

That is where the factory comes in. AEP's order works out at about US$2.94 million a megawatt, if it covers the full 900 MW; if AEP took less, the price per megawatt is higher and the gigawatts needed fewer. At that price, with products at 87.5 per cent of revenue, US$10.0 billion is about 3.0 GW shipped in 2028. Bloom's Fremont plant makes about 1 GW a year and is doubling to 2 GW by the end of 2026, with room on the site for about 5 GW. These megawatt figures are my conversions; Bloom does not report megawatts shipped.

**Where I differ.** Not on demand, which is real, but on what Bloom keeps from it. The price treats the scarcity of grid power as though it lifts Bloom's prices as well as its volumes. Two years of product margins say it does not. So Bloom's earnings grow at the pace of its factory and its financing partners, and both are visible and finite. My base case reaches about 2.6 GW in 2028, which already needs Fremont to grow beyond its planned 2 GW.

The grid is also slowly getting faster. In January FERC approved a Southwest Power Pool route that studies large new loads, with their own generation, within 90 days. That is one region, and the queues remain long. But it is the direction the claim's falsifier warned about.

## Valuation, with the working

I value Bloom on non-GAAP earnings per share, the basis on which it guides, twelve months out. By October 2027 the market will price 2028, so that is the year I value. Non-GAAP figures leave out stock-based pay, which was US$56 million in the second quarter, almost a quarter of non-GAAP operating income. Second quarter earnings were US$0.78 a share on that basis and US$0.62 under GAAP.

**Dilution, counted properly.** I use fully diluted shares, not the 294.5 million outstanding. The US$2.5 billion of 0% notes due 2030 convert at US$194.97, into 12.8 million shares, or up to 19.6 million after a takeover. The remaining 2029 notes add 1.3 million. Staff awards added 16.0 million to the second quarter's diluted count. That gives 325 million, and I add 1.5 per cent a year for new awards. Oracle's warrant, over 3.5 million shares at US$113.28, was exercised in May for 2.2 million shares, which are already counted. Its value of US$324 million comes off revenue as Oracle's systems are delivered.

Gross margin rises slowly, in line with a flat product margin and a service business still improving. Bloom pays almost no tax today because of past losses; I assume a non-GAAP rate of 10 per cent in 2027 and 15 per cent in 2028. My 2027 earnings sit 2 per cent above the consensus, and my 2028 earnings 14 per cent below.

**The multiple.** I use 40 times 2028 earnings for the base case. GE Vernova, which makes the gas turbines Bloom competes with, trades at 47 times forward earnings, and Vertiv at 32, according to stockanalysis.com on 6 October. Bloom is growing faster than both, but sells to fewer and larger customers. One customer took about 73 per cent of second quarter revenue, and two took 44 and 21 per cent of the first half, as Bloom's amended 10-Q states.

## Risks, and what would change my view

**The volume keeps surprising.** This is the bull case and the main risk to a view that leans short. Bloom raised its 2026 revenue guide by about 27 per cent at the midpoint in five months. Another step like that, with Fremont expanding quickly, puts 2028 close to the bull case, a 38 per cent rise.

**The gap closes.** The bear case and the claim's own falsifier. Faster interconnection or more turbine capacity shrinks the window Bloom sells into. The Southwest Power Pool route is a small first sign.

**Few, large buyers.** In 2025, 44 per cent of revenue went to related parties, mainly joint ventures with Brookfield in which Bloom holds stakes of 9.9 to 15 per cent. Bloom's ability to ship depends on financing as much as on orders.

**Gas and scandium.** The boxes need a gas supply and scandium for their ceramic cells. A supply or permit shock would slow deployments. The short report and a securities lawsuit that followed it are outstanding.

**The notes.** Holders of the 0% notes can convert early only after a quarter in which the shares closed above US$253.46 on 20 of the last 30 trading days. On my count of Yahoo closes the third quarter managed 17, so early conversion looks unlikely this quarter. Bloom may settle in cash, shares or both.

**What would change my view.** Above about US$322, my base case would sit 25 per cent below the price, which would make a short worth proposing. A 2027 guide in February of less than 40 per cent growth would do the same. Below about US$210, the base case would be 15 per cent above the price. Product gross margin of 38 per cent or more in two quarters running would show Bloom pricing the time it sells, and would move me towards the bull case.

The third quarter results have no date yet. Last year Bloom announced the date on 9 October and reported on 28 October. Fremont is due to reach 2 GW a year by the end of 2026, and the fourth quarter results in February bring the 2027 guide and the year-end backlog.

## Conclusion

The September piece argued that Bloom sells time, not electricity. The buyers prove it: Oracle, AEP and Brookfield are committing billions for speed. What I got wrong was how Bloom is paid for it. Product margin has been flat at about 35 per cent while revenue tripled, so the premium arrives as volume, and volume is set by a factory.

At US$286.65 the price needs about 3 GW shipped in 2028, half as much again as the plant's planned capacity. My call is no call, with medium conviction, leaning short. The base case is worth about US$241 and the range runs from US$83 to US$394. I would revisit above about US$322 or below about US$210. The draft is wrong if 2027 revenue reaches US$7 billion with a gross margin of 36 per cent or more, or the shares close above US$360 or below US$200 by October 2027.
