SpaceX's shares closed at US$134.72 on 30 October 2026, a few cents below the US$135 they were sold at in June. At that price the company is worth about US$1.83 trillion, or US$1.78 trillion after US$51 billion of net cash.

My journal piece of 2 November argued that data centres in orbit are a contest of weight. In orbit the power is nearly free, but heat leaves only by glowing off radiators, and every kilogram must be launched. The claim: by the end of 2028 no operator will report a compute satellite in service at SpaceX's own target of 100 kilowatts per tonne launched, and no rocket company will publish a price at or below US$200 a kilogram.

This note asks what that means for the shares. SpaceX does not report orbital compute as a business. So the useful question is how much of the price the three businesses it does report can explain, and how much is left for orbit and everything else.

## The thesis

Think of a sealed vacuum flask on a sunny windowsill, with a laptop inside. The sun charges it for nothing, but the flask traps the heat, so the laptop needs a black fin poking out to glow the heat away. Every fin goes up by courier, charged by the kilogram. That was the piece. A buyer of SpaceX is buying the courier, the phone network and a large ground data centre business, and is also being asked to pay for fins nobody has yet weighed in orbit.

SpaceX reports three segments. **Space** is launch: Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, Dragon and the development of Starship. **Connectivity** is Starlink. **Super Intelligence**, SI, renamed from AI this quarter, holds xAI, X and, since 14 August, Cursor, the coding tool SpaceX bought for an implied US$60 billion. It rents computing capacity to other companies and sells Grok, advertising and software.

The third quarter shows where the money now is. Revenue was US$14.6 billion, up 161 per cent. SI brought in US$7.6 billion, up from US$2.6 billion the quarter before, against US$5.2 billion for Starlink and US$1.8 billion for Space. SI's Segment Adjusted EBITDA, SpaceX's measure of earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, share awards and deal costs, was US$5.0 billion. One customer, which SpaceX names only as Customer B and places in SI, supplied 34.5 per cent of all revenue.

Orbital compute appears in none of this. The prospectus of 12 June says SpaceX expects to begin deploying AI compute satellites "as early as 2028" and targets more than 100 kilowatts of compute per metric ton. The 10-Q for the third quarter, filed on 22 October, does not mention it.

SpaceX's own Starmind page, read on 2 November, does. It describes its AI satellite, AI1: up to 250 kilowatts of compute at peak and 175 on average, a 160 square metre liquid radiator, a 210 kilowatt solar array and a "vehicle efficiency" of "75 kW / ton". The page does not say whether that is compute or solar power, or which ton. Read as compute per metric ton, it is a quarter short of the 100 kilowatt target the claim tests. On average compute it implies about 2.3 tonnes a satellite, on my arithmetic. A radiator of that area at the space station's 14 kilograms a square metre would weigh about 2.2 tonnes on its own.

The launch side tells the same story from SpaceX's own books. In the first nine months of 2026 its launch customers paid US$2,108 million for 251 tonnes of payload. That is about US$8,400 for each kilogram they flew, on my arithmetic, 42 times the US$200 the claim tests. This is revenue per kilogram, not a list price, and Starship has so far flown only SpaceX's own payloads. But nothing in the record points to a US$200 price soon.

**The first satellites fly in 2027.** On the third quarter call of 20 October, Gwynne Shotwell, SpaceX's president, said: "We'll attempt an on-orbit refueling early next year, and we'll begin flying Mobile V2 and Starmind satellites in 2027", in SpaceX's own transcript. Its Starmind page plans "thousands of AI satellites starting as soon as late 2027". That is a year ahead of the prospectus.

No satellite in service has yet shown what a kilowatt in orbit weighs. The 75 kW per ton on the AI1 page is a design figure, with its basis unstated. The claim stands, and the two facts cut both ways: the first satellites may fly a year earlier, so the claim may be tested sooner, but the design figure SpaceX has shown, read as compute per metric ton, sits below the target the claim names.

## What the market prices in, and where I differ

At US$134.72 SpaceX trades at 33.4 times the 2027 consensus of US$4.03 a share, and its enterprise value is 9.9 times 2027 consensus revenue of US$180 billion, both from Yahoo Finance on 2 November. Those estimates span US$72 billion to US$290 billion of revenue, a sign of how little anyone can see.

The guide is steep. SpaceX expects US$33 to 37 billion of revenue in the first quarter of 2027 alone, against US$14.6 billion in the third quarter of 2026. Its finance chief said it remains on track for US$100 billion of annualised revenue by the end of 2026, and expects nameplate compute of 2.5 to 3 gigawatts by then. Elon Musk said he would be surprised by less than 13 gigawatts by the end of 2027. Fourth quarter capital spending is guided at US$35 to 40 billion.

I take most of that at face value, and value the three businesses on my 2027 estimates. They come to about US$1.37 trillion. The market's enterprise value is US$1.78 trillion. **The difference, about US$404 billion or 23 per cent, is what the price pays for beyond the businesses SpaceX reports.** That is Starship beyond its launch revenue, Starlink Mobile, the Terafab chip plant, and orbital compute, which is the largest of them and the one SpaceX puts at the centre of its story.

Put the gap in the piece's own units. My SI value works out at about US$65 billion per gigawatt of the 13 gigawatts Musk expects by the end of 2027. At that rate US$404 billion buys about 6.2 gigawatts of orbital compute, before any discount for time. At AI1's 175 kilowatts that is about 36,000 satellites. At AI1's 75 kilowatts per ton, read as compute per metric ton, it is about 83,000 tonnes in orbit, about 38 times everything SpaceX launched in 2025.

So here is where I differ. If the claim holds, the satellites SpaceX flies by the end of 2028 will be heavier per kilowatt than its target, and launch will still cost far more than US$200 a kilogram. Then orbital compute cannot earn its share of the gap within the horizon anyone can value. I credit US$100 billion to all of SpaceX's unreported options together. That leaves about US$304 billion, roughly US$22 a share, which the price asks orbit and the other bets to earn.

What the claim does not do is make the shares a short. The gap is real, but the price sits about 20 per cent above my base, not twice it, and the three businesses are growing faster than any I value elsewhere. The bigger swing factor over twelve months is the ground business: whether SI's revenue reaches the guide, at what margin, and whether a 34.5 per cent customer stays.

Space is valued on revenue, because Starship spending makes its earnings close to zero. I add net cash of US$51.0 billion at 30 September and divide by 13,581 million shares, the Class A and Class B count at 15 October. I do not deduct the fourth quarter's capital spending or the US$40 billion Apollo GPU lease SpaceX committed to on 24 October, because both buy capacity that my 2027 estimates assume.

## Risks, and what would change my view

**SI misses the ramp.** The guide asks revenue to more than double between the third quarter of 2026 and the first of 2027. Musk said SpaceX aims to "rent at effectively the spot-market rate" rather than sign long-term deals. If prices for rented computing fall as capacity floods in, revenue and margin fall together. That is the bear case.

**One customer.** Customer B was 34.5 per cent of third quarter revenue. One customer also held 27.7 per cent of receivables on 30 September. SpaceX does not name it.

**Cash and debt.** SpaceX spent US$48.1 billion on capital in nine months and guides US$35 to 40 billion more in the fourth quarter. It has issued US$25 billion of bonds and signed a binding commitment to a US$40 billion GPU lease, expected to close before the year end. A slower ramp would leave that spending earning less than my base assumes.

**Control and a Tesla deal.** Musk held about 82 per cent of the votes after the IPO, so SpaceX is a controlled company. Both boards have formed special committees to study a combination with Tesla. The terms of any deal could move value either way, and any deal also needs the approval of SpaceX's controlling shareholder, Musk.

**Supply.** Under the prospectus, the last of the 180 day lock-up ends on 8 December 2026, when all remaining shares under it may be sold. Musk's shares stay locked until June 2027, and other holders' extended lock-up runs until after the second quarter 2027 results; together these are more than 63 per cent of the shares outstanding before the IPO.

**Orbit itself.** If the claim fails, and a satellite flies at 100 kilowatts per tonne by 2028 or launch reaches US$200 a kilogram, the gap could be earned. That is the bull case's option value.

**What would change my view.** A price above about US$150 would put my base value 25 per cent below it, towards a short. A price below about US$98 would put it 15 per cent above, towards a long. A weighed AI satellite in service at 100 kilowatts per tonne would break the claim and lift the option value.

Starship Flight 15, with an attempt to catch both stages, is due around November. SpaceX has not announced the date of its fourth quarter results.

## Conclusion

The November piece argued that in orbit the heat is the bill. SpaceX's own disclosures since say the same: its first AI satellite is designed at a stated 75 kW per ton against a 100 kilowatt target, and its launch customers paid about US$8,400 a kilogram this year against a US$200 test. The first satellites are due in 2027.

For the shares, orbit is a gap, not the whole price. The three businesses SpaceX reports explain about three quarters of the enterprise value. The rest, about US$404 billion, rests on bets that earn nothing yet, with orbital compute the largest. My base value of US$112 sits 17 per cent below US$134.72, inside the no-call band. My call is NO CALL, with medium conviction: no position, because a calibrated no call beats a forced short on a stock with under five months of trading and a lock-up ending on 8 December. I would revisit above about US$150 or below about US$98, or if a weighed AI satellite flies at the target.
